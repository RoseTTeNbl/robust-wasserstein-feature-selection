\section{One question, two kinds of evidence}
We want to choose a small set of measurements that makes two uncertain class distributions distinguishable. The proposed selector minimizes a two-class Wasserstein robust testing risk under a feature budget. The theory explains how a continuous test problem can supply global lower cuts for the discrete selection problem. This report asks whether that construction is computationally useful and whether the resulting measurements are useful on new data.

These are separate questions. An accurate global certificate establishes how well a particular empirical objective has been optimized. It does not establish that the objective is the best selection principle for a population, that the chosen radius covers that population, or that a two-sample test has high power. Conversely, a selector can perform well on held-out observations without providing a global certificate for its training criterion.

We therefore organize the study around two evidence lines. The \emph{computational line} checks the finite formulation and its cuts, then compares original-objective upper bounds, global lower bounds, time, search effort, and unsuccessful runs. The \emph{statistical line} uses independent samples to assess equality-test calibration and power, signal recovery, prediction, and response to specified distribution shifts. A common downstream evaluation prevents differences in prediction architecture from being mistaken for differences in selected coordinates.

The primary comparisons do not establish better statistical selection from the robust objective: mean, variance, and dependence results expose weak recovery or power relative to other selectors. The stronger risk-scaled gate is also not uniformly faster in the current master implementation. A separately declared exploratory supplement retains the full dual fraction to address the observed computational limitation. The main results and this supplement are reported separately.

\subsection{Why this evaluation follows the local literature}
The experimental sections and relevant appendices of the source papers were read before the protocol and implementation. Wang, Dey, and Xie \cite{wang} motivate independent selection and final testing, power paired with null rejection, and false-discovery/non-discovery proportions. Xie, Gao, and Xie \cite{xie} motivate small training samples, nonlinear distribution differences, and independent classification error. Gao et al.\ \cite{gao} motivate inspecting uncertainty radii. Swaroop, Krishnamurti, and Wilder \cite{swaroop} motivate a separate downstream-training split and a common prediction pipeline. Bertsimas, Cory-Wright, and Pauphilet \cite{unified} motivate computational ablations and explicit time-limited outcomes.

We transfer these evaluation ideas to our task. We do not identify the two class labels with the multiple deployment populations in Swaroop et al., or relabel sequential detection delay as fixed-sample testing power. Our synthetic mechanisms and sample sizes are specified adaptations. The baseline section records where a mathematical reconstruction differs from inspected author code.

\section{What the algorithm does, from one support to global search}
\subsection{The original testing target}
Class $k\in\{0,1\}$ supplies observations $x_{ki}\in\R^D$ with positive weights $q_{ki}$ summing to one within that class. Its empirical measure is $\widehat P_k=\sum_iq_{ki}\delta_{x_{ki}}$. The true class distribution is protected only if it belongs to the stated ball $W_1(P_k,\widehat P_k)\le\theta_k$, with strictly positive radius $\theta_k$. The ground cost is L1 on the full standardized observation space.

A binary mask $z$ selects coordinates $S(z)=\{j:z_j=1\}$. A positive score $g$ favors class zero. The class-zero and class-one losses are respectively $\Phi(-g)$ and $\Phi(g)$. Our primary loss is half hinge, $\Phi(v)=(1+v)_+/2$; logistic loss is used in secondary correctness checks. The objective is
\begin{equation}
\OPT=\min_{\substack{z\in\{0,1\}^D\\\one^\top z\le d}} V(z),\qquad
V(z)=\inf_{g=g(x_{S(z)})}
\left[\sup_{P_0}\E_{P_0}\Phi(-g)+\sup_{P_1}\E_{P_1}\Phi(g)\right].
\label{eq:original}
\end{equation}
The two suprema use their respective Wasserstein balls. A test may ignore an additional observed coordinate, so an optimal support can be padded to exactly $d$ coordinates. This justifies the full-budget equality in the implemented master. It does not establish that every selected variable has unique population relevance.

Half-hinge scores can be clipped to $[-1,1]$. Writing $\pi_0=(1+g)/2$, the losses become $1-\pi_0$ and $\pi_0$, the two randomized error probabilities. Thus $V$ is a \emph{sum} of classwise risks; its equal-prior balanced average is $V/2$. The uninformative test has risk $B=1$. Logistic has the corresponding constant-test risk $2\log2$.

\subsection{One fixed support becomes a convex test problem}
Pool all source observations as targets $t_1,\ldots,t_N$. Let $\Delta_{kimj}=|x_{ki,j}-t_{m,j}|$. The fixed-support oracle solves
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
V(z)=\min_{a,u,\lambda}\quad&J(a,\lambda)
 :=\sum_{ki}q_{ki}a_{ki}+\sum_k\theta_k\lambda_k\\
\text{subject to}\quad&a_{ki}+\lambda_k\sum_jz_j\Delta_{kimj}
 \ge\Phi(\sigma_ku_m),\qquad\lambda_k\ge0,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:finite}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_0=-1$, $\sigma_1=1$. The score $u_m$ is the test value at target $m$. The potential $a_{ki}$ bounds a source atom's loss, and $\lambda_k$ prices additional transport. The potentials and scores are free variables; self-target constraints make feasible potentials nonnegative. For half hinge, using $r_m=(1+u_m)/2\in[0,1]$ makes this an ordinary linear program.

The finite pool is exact for the stated two losses and ambient-space assumptions. The theory note proves this by finite transport-LP duality in one direction and a constructive extension to every observation in the other. In particular, the test is not deployed by an arbitrary interpolation of pool scores. It uses compatible whole-space loss envelopes,
\begin{equation}
E_k(x)=\min_i\left\{a_{ki}+\lambda_k
\sum_{j\in S(z)}|x_{ki,j}-x_j|\right\}.
\label{eq:envelopes}
\end{equation}
For half hinge, take the midpoint of
$[\max(0,1-E_0(x)),\min(1,E_1(x))]$ as $\pi_0(x)$. The interval is nonempty by the finite constraints and the triangle inequality. Integrating these envelope bounds over any admissible transport gives classwise risk at most $q_k^\top a_k+\theta_k\lambda_k$. A repaired primal solution therefore supplies a feasible-test \emph{upper} bound.

\subsection{Why a support evaluation can inform every other support}
Introduce a two-class coordinate-price block $w_j=(w_{0j},w_{1j})$. An inactive coordinate forces $w_j=0$; an active coordinate allows $0\le w_{kj}\le\lambda_k$. Since distance entries are nonnegative, saturating an active price to $\lambda_k$ preserves loss feasibility. The logical-coordinate formulation therefore has the same binary optimum as \eqref{eq:finite}.

Nonnegative potentials imply $\theta^\top\lambda\le J\le B$ at an optimum. Applying the grouped logical-constraint idea of \cite{unified} yields a shared fixed gate $\theta^\top w_j\le Bz_j$. Our problem-specific strengthening uses the risk being minimized: $J\le\eta$ and $\theta^\top w_j\le\eta z_j$. For fixed $z$ this remains a convex oracle, and it preserves the exact binary objective.

The oracle's dual allocates source mass through $\Gamma$, measures unavoidable target overlap through $\Hconf(\Gamma)$, and covers coordinatewise transport beyond class allocations with nonnegative prices $\mu_j$. These expanded certificates need not be admissible original adversarial distributions. Scaling their mass and prices gives a normalization $\tau+\widehat\mu^\top z=1$. The value function can then be written over a support-independent certificate domain as
\begin{equation}
R(z)=\max_c\frac{\Hconf(\Gamma_c)}{1+\mu_c^\top z},
\qquad R(z)=V(z)\text{ for binary }z.
\label{eq:rational}
\end{equation}
Each fixed-certificate fraction is convex on $[0,1]^D$. An oracle certificate generated at $z^t$ therefore gives a global tangent. In normalized quantities, with its own value $v_t$, this is
\begin{equation}
\eta\ge L_t(z):=v_t-v_t(\widehat\mu^t)^\top(z-z^t).
\label{eq:tangent}
\end{equation}
A feasible nonoptimal certificate gives a valid, possibly non-touching cut. Its own recomputed dual value is essential; substituting a primal upper objective would invalidate that argument.

\subsection{One complete oracle/master cycle}
The master minimizes $\eta$ over binary full-budget masks, $\eta\ge0$, and all accumulated cuts \eqref{eq:tangent}. Its global solver bound is a lower bound $L$ for \eqref{eq:original}. The best feasible original test has an upper bound $U$. Consequently,
\begin{equation}
L\le\OPT\le U,\qquad
U-L\le10^{-6}+10^{-5}|U|
\label{eq:stopping}
\end{equation}
is the declared numerical stopping criterion.

The loop is straightforward. Start from the same marginal-MMD screened support for every optimization variant. Refit every test variable and dual variable at that support. Keep its feasible-test upper bound and its global lower cut. Solve the master, obtain its next support, and repeat. Optional fractional root calls add lower cuts before integer search. They do not provide deployable-support upper bounds. Every new candidate is a complete continuous reoptimization; the method does not mix fixed historical transports as a substitute.

If the master is interrupted, its best feasible master objective is merely the value of a lower model at one support. The implementation uses the solver's \emph{global bound}, not that incumbent objective, to update $L$. If a numerical oracle leaves a gap and the master revisits a cached support, the solver reports an accuracy limit. Exact finite termination is a theorem for tight oracle cuts and exact master solves; it is not inferred from repetition in floating-point computation.

\section{Data are separated before any method is evaluated}
\subsection{Three disjoint roles within each training seed}
Feature selection, downstream fitting, and final evaluation use separate samples. The selection split is the only split available to any selector. Its pooled mean and sample standard deviation define a common coordinate transformation, with a small scale floor for constant columns. That transformation is frozen and applied to every later split and every selector.

The downstream split fits two common heads on each selected support: the robust half-hinge envelope test and an RBF support-vector classifier. A selector cannot revise its support using this split. Final classification observations then assess those heads. Separate fresh two-sample batches assess equality testing; their labels are unavailable during selection and bandwidth fitting.

This separation makes the comparisons interpretable. Conditional on independently chosen measurements, the final permutation test has a fixed support and kernel. The common heads then assess every support through the same prediction pipeline, so differences in architecture cannot explain a selection comparison.

\subsection{Four mechanisms ask four different questions}
The statistical experiment has $D=12$, three designated signal coordinates, and feature budget $d=3$. All other coordinates are independent standard Gaussian noise. Feature positions are shuffled independently for each training seed. Recovery is evaluated in the underlying semantic coordinate indexing, so the shuffling does not artificially destroy stability.

The dependence case is particularly informative. Every individual coordinate has the same population distribution in both classes, so marginal MMD screening and an additive sum of marginal kernels have no population signal. Sampling fluctuation can nevertheless make their selected supports look informative in training. The held-out common RBF test can detect joint differences when the selected set contains useful signal coordinates. This separates the ability to choose a support from the ability of its downstream test to exploit interactions.

The mixture uses one shared sign per observation, not independently sampled signs for each coordinate. That distinction creates dependence among signal coordinates. It follows the role of equal-mean nonlinear alternatives in the source literature, without claiming a literal replay of the original high-dimensional experiment.

Thirty independent training seeds are predeclared. Each provides the three splits in Figure~\ref{fig:split}. The primary radii are $\theta_0=\theta_1=0.25$ in the common standardized L1 geometry. Radii and scaling are not recomputed for individual supports. Otherwise two supports would be compared under different uncertainty sets, changing the discrete objective.

\subsection{Two public real datasets}
The UCI Wisconsin Diagnostic Breast Cancer dataset has 569 records and 30 numeric features; identifiers and diagnosis are excluded from the feature matrix. Class zero is benign and class one malignant. The UCI Ionosphere dataset has 351 records and 34 features; the label is excluded, with good returns mapped to zero and bad returns to one. The public source pages and persistent dataset identifiers are given in the references \cite{wdbc,ionosphere}.

For each of 20 stratified split seeds, 20 records per class are allocated to selection and another disjoint 20 per class to downstream fitting. Remaining records are held out for classification. The budget is three, the radii are $0.25$, and robust global selection receives the same 30-second cap. Scaling is again fitted only on the selection records. Dataset downloads, label mappings, excluded fields, licensing, and file hashes are preserved in the source manifest.

Repeated splits reuse a finite dataset. Their mean and standard deviation describe split variability. Neither dataset has a known ground-truth feature support for this task, so synthetic recovery measures are omitted. These are methodological examples rather than clinical validation.

\section{Each reported metric answers a stated question}
\subsection{Did the algorithm optimize its training objective?}
The final upper bound is the objective of an independently feasible original test. The final lower bound comes from the global master solver. Their absolute difference measures unresolved optimization error; the normalized difference uses the reported objective scale. The numerical solved fraction counts runs meeting \eqref{eq:stopping} out of all scheduled runs, including failures and time limits in the denominator.

Runtime measures total elapsed selection time, not only the last solver call. We also separate oracle time from master time, count all oracle calls including root points, and count distinct evaluated integer supports. A method may visit fewer supports while taking longer because each master search or oracle call is more expensive. Reporting these quantities together explains a runtime change instead of attributing it solely to a stronger relaxation.

The root bound measures what the finite set of fractional cuts has certified before integer search. The computational comparison separately examines the complete oracle value and this finite-cut master bound, since they measure different parts of the search.

\subsection{Did the selected measurements support a calibrated equality test?}
An equality test asks whether two new sample groups have the same distribution. This differs from labeling one observation. Given a frozen support, the common test computes the unbiased RBF maximum mean discrepancy statistic,
\begin{equation}
T=\frac{\sum_{i\ne i'}k(X_i,X_{i'})}{m(m-1)}
 +\frac{\sum_{h\ne h'}k(Y_h,Y_{h'})}{n(n-1)}
 -\frac{2\sum_{i,h}k(X_i,Y_h)}{mn}.
\label{eq:mmd}
\end{equation}
The kernel uses all selected coordinates jointly. Its bandwidth is a median squared distance fitted on pooled selection data, then frozen. The final Gram matrix depends on the observations and frozen kernel, not on how the test labels are shuffled.

Generate 199 random fixed-size label assignments. The p-value is
\begin{equation}
p=\frac{1+\#\{T_{\mathrm{perm}}\ge T_{\mathrm{obs}}\}}{200}.
\label{eq:pvalue}
\end{equation}
Reject at $p\le0.05$. Under the null, both fresh groups are drawn independently from class zero. Exchangeability conditional on the independently chosen support motivates this calibration. \emph{Null rejection} is the empirical probability of rejection in these null batches; \emph{power} is rejection in alternative batches. High power is convincing only alongside null rejection close to the nominal level. A small null excess should be interpreted with finite-repeat uncertainty, not automatically as a structural calibration failure.

Within each seed we average its twenty rejection indicators. Across-seed summaries and intervals use those seed-level fractions. The twenty conditional batches make each trained selector's assessment more precise; they do not create twenty independently trained selectors. The robust-risk theorem does not itself imply level-$0.05$ power. This common test evaluates the utility of the support, rather than claiming a new theorem connecting the two objectives.

\subsection{Did the selector recover designated signal coordinates?}
For selected set $S$ and generating signal set $T_*$, report
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{FDP}=\frac{|S\setminus T_*|}{\max(1,|S|)},\qquad
\operatorname{NDP}=\frac{|T_*\setminus S|}{|T_*|}.
\label{eq:recovery}
\end{equation}
False-discovery proportion measures the fraction of purchased measurements outside the designated signal. Non-discovery proportion measures the signal fraction missed. Lower is better for both. Precision is $1-\operatorname{FDP}$, recall is $1-\operatorname{NDP}$, and F1 combines them as a harmonic mean. With equal selected and true support sizes, FDP and NDP coincide, while precision, recall and F1 all equal the recovered signal fraction. These equivalent views are retained for continuity with the source literature and do not supply independent findings.

Across-run stability is the mean pairwise Jaccard similarity $|S_a\cap S_b|/|S_a\cup S_b|$, using semantic coordinates in shuffled synthetic problems. Stability and recovery are different: a selector can consistently choose the same unhelpful variables. The designated changed-coordinate set is an interpretive reference, not a universal definition of a Bayes-optimal support in more general correlated data.

\subsection{Did new individual observations get distinguished?}
The common robust head gives a probability $\pi_0(x)$ of accepting class zero. On final observations, its randomized class-zero error is the mean of $1-\pi_0$ and its randomized class-one error is the mean of $\pi_0$. Randomized balanced error is half their sum:
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{BER}_{\mathrm{rand}}
=\frac12\left[\frac1{m}\sum_i(1-\pi_0(X_i))
 +\frac1n\sum_h\pi_0(Y_h)\right].
\label{eq:ber}
\end{equation}
This equals the empirical half-hinge surrogate average. The surrogate sum is twice that value. These names describe the same measurement and its normalization, rather than independent successes.

For deterministic prediction, threshold $\pi_0$ at $0.5$, assigning ties to class zero. Its classwise errors and balanced average are reported separately. The randomized certificate does not directly bound that thresholded error. Area under the ROC curve uses $1-\pi_0$ as the class-one score and measures ranking across thresholds. Predictive log loss assesses probability sharpness; its probabilities are clipped only for that metric to avoid numerical infinities. A half-hinge-trained head has not thereby solved a logistic training objective.

The common RBF support-vector classifier supplies another balanced-error and ranking evaluation. It uses $C=1$ and a median bandwidth fitted only on downstream-training data. Agreement across the two heads is more persuasive evidence about the support than a gain confined to the envelope construction.

\subsection{What the uncertainty bars mean}
Synthetic figures use 95\% bootstrap intervals over independent training seeds, with 2,000 fixed-seed resamples. Test replicates are averaged inside their training seed first. Method differences are paired within seed because methods share data, transformations, test batches, and permutation seeds. Real-data summaries use mean $\pm$ standard deviation over repeated splits. The report does not treat a scan across many metrics and panels as a formal significance test, or reinterpret an optimization gap as sampling uncertainty.

\section{Which baselines are reproduced, and what is held common?}
All budget-matched selectors see exactly the same selection split and choose three coordinates in the primary statistical experiment. The subsequent heads, test data, preprocessing, and evaluation rules are common. Comparisons therefore target support utility. Each selector's criterion is explained below so that similarly named methods do not conceal different mathematical tasks.

\subsection{Risk-based global selection and robust greedy}
The proposed global method uses the exact two-class robust risk and global cuts from Section~2. A time-limited run contributes its best feasible support and actual bounds. Robust greedy shares the same loss and radius but adds the coordinate producing the smallest fully refitted robust risk at each forward stage. Its candidates are complete fixed-support solves. Greedy can miss a useful joint combination because an irreversible first choice constrains later stages. This baseline isolates the practical value of global search while holding the criterion fixed.

If greedy reaches its common wall limit before a full stage completes, it keeps its last completed support and fills the remaining budget with the predeclared marginal ranking. That outcome is labeled as a time-limited greedy adaptation. It is not silently recorded as an exact greedy trajectory.

\subsection{Marginal screening, sparse logistic ranking, and random selection}
Univariate screening ranks unbiased MMD values of one-dimensional RBF coordinate kernels. It is simple, transparent, and can perform strongly when marginal differences exist. It has no population signal in the equal-marginal dependence mechanism. It is not a reproduction of Wang et al.'s optimized weighted kernel.

Sparse logistic ranking fits the fixed L1 logistic baseline family, with liblinear and $C=1$, on selection data. The largest absolute coefficients provide the common feature budget. Training variance and feature index resolve coefficient ties. Ranking a regularized fit to obtain exactly $d$ coordinates is an explicit adaptation. A linear predictor is naturally challenged by some equal-mean mechanisms; this does not make its performance a universal statement about sparse classification.

Random selection chooses a seeded uniform budget-sized subset. It supplies a negative control under the same evaluation. On synthetic data, the known signal support and an all-feature test are additional diagnostics. The former is unavailable to a real selector; the latter buys more measurements. They are not budget-matched deployable competitors.

\subsection{Wang additive-MMD: a criterion reproduction with disclosed differences}
Wang, Dey, and Xie \cite{wang} optimize weights of a sparse additive kernel,
\begin{equation}
K_\omega(x,y)=\sum_j\omega_j
\exp\!\left[-\frac{(x_j-y_j)^2}{2b_j^2}\right],\qquad
\|\omega\|_2=1,\quad\|\omega\|_0\le d.
\label{eq:additive}
\end{equation}
``Linear'' here means linear in kernel weights, not a raw dot-product kernel. Coordinate bandwidths follow the inspected revision convention: median squared cross-class selection distances, with factor one and a numerical floor for constant coordinates.

From paired coordinate-kernel contrasts, form the unbiased mean vector $t$ and the row-covariance variance estimator $\widehat\Sigma$. The paper's mean-minus-variance criterion is
\begin{equation}
\max_{\|\omega\|_2=1,\ \|\omega\|_0\le d}
t^\top\omega-0.1\,\omega^\top\widehat\Sigma\omega.
\label{eq:wang}
\end{equation}
For $D\le12$, the implementation enumerates candidate supports and globally solves the sphere-constrained quadratic weight problem on each support. It includes the trust-region hard case rather than relying on a local gradient optimizer. For larger $D$, a documented forward/swap search optimizes the same criterion and is explicitly marked approximate.

The inspected official revision returns a \emph{positive} variance matrix before an eigenvalue shift. The inspected optimizer maximizes the resulting positive quadratic term; it does not negate that matrix. The combination therefore rewards variance, whereas \eqref{eq:wang} penalizes it. Our primary baseline is labeled \textbf{Wang additive-MMD, paper objective}; it is not called a literal replay of the revision script. The official source commit and coefficient tests preserve the discrepancy for inspection \cite{wangcode}.

The source testing routine also recomputes a cross-class bandwidth using final labels. Our native additive diagnostic freezes the training bandwidth and uses the common plus-one permutation construction, preserving the independent-test calibration logic. Optimized negative weights are retained where permitted by the criterion. Such a signed weighted statistic can be permutation-calibrated when frozen independently, but it should not be called a positive-definite-kernel MMD unless its weights are nonnegative.

The \emph{common-support} test discards those weights and evaluates the selected coordinates with the same joint RBF statistic as every selector. The \emph{native additive} diagnostic uses the learned weights. Their different labels distinguish support utility from a complete learned testing pipeline. Neither is presented as a reproduced numerical table from the source paper.

\subsection{Why some nearby papers do not become automatic competitors}
Swaroop et al.'s selector protects several specified downstream populations. Treating class zero and class one as those populations creates one-class groups and changes its problem. We adopt its evaluation separation without claiming to reproduce its group objective. Similarly, Hanada et al.\ \cite{hanada} retain a safe union of potentially useful variables under adaptation; this need not satisfy a fixed acquisition budget. Neither method is silently transformed into a budget-matched comparator.

\section{Correctness evidence before performance claims}
The mathematical unit checks compare the original fixed-support primal with an independent source-marginal, total-transport-budget dual. This reference contains no coordinate gates, normalized mass scale, beta allocation, or mu prices. Agreement therefore tests the original objective rather than merely comparing two versions of the same matrix assembly.

Twenty random small instances use $D=6$, three or four atoms per class, unequal positive radii, nonuniform positive weights, and budgets one through three. All supports are enumerated. Fixed gate primals, gate duals, original transport values, and every cross-support cut are compared. Random fractional points additionally check gate ordering and tangent validity. The suite includes duplicates, unequal class sizes, zero/full budgets, and analytic one-coordinate curves. Logistic uses smaller independently interpretable checks and an exponential-cone solver.

Deployment checks query the whole-space envelope, verify its probability interval, alter unselected coordinates, and move source atoms with an explicitly budget-feasible coupling. Additional experiment checks verify split independence, semantic recovery under shuffled positions, scalar kernel/variance coefficients, global weight fitting including the hard case, direct unbiased MMD arithmetic, plus-one p-values, and risk normalization. A simulated interrupted master confirms that its feasible incumbent is not substituted for its global lower bound.

These tests provide evidence about the actual implementation. The derivations establish the mathematical statements under their assumptions. Ordinary floating-point repair and small conservative margins are numerical safeguards, not a directed-rounding theorem for arbitrary ill-conditioned inputs. The report consequently uses declared numerical tolerances when describing optimality.

\section{Computational results: stronger values, actual search costs}
\subsection{A paired comparison with the same objective}
The primary comparison uses fixed box gates, shared fixed-triangle gates, risk-scaled gates without root cuts, and risk-scaled gates with five root rounds. Small cases also use enumeration. All compare the same integer risk, common initial support, radii, stopping rule, hardware, and 30-second limit. A solved gate variant should agree with independently completed enumeration, up to declared tolerance; a different objective is not a benefit of the gate.

\subsection{Why the present master can be expensive}
Each SciPy integer-master call starts a new mixed-integer search. The interface does not embed these cuts as lazy constraints in one continuing branch-and-bound tree. The reference paper discusses that implementation route, but it is not exposed by the present high-level interface. Restarting many master searches creates overhead even when the continuous oracle is inexpensive.

Moreover, the master retains tangents, not the entire function in \eqref{eq:rational}. A tangent of a larger convex function can lie below a tangent of a smaller function away from the anchor. Theoretical pointwise dominance of complete extensions does not remove this limitation. The timing decomposition and number of nearly exhaustive support visits are the appropriate evidence for this explanation; they do not prove that all possible exact implementations would have the same bottleneck.

\subsection{Numerical master failures and the documented remedy}
Original runs exposed reproducible status-4 errors in the numerical master presolve. These outcomes must remain visible. The remedy retries the \emph{identical} master model with presolve disabled, preserving the objective, constraints, cuts, and integer domain. The original attempt is retained in diagnostics and its time is charged to the same wall budget. This is a numerical implementation remedy rather than a new algorithmic treatment.

\subsection{Two different shifted-data calculations}
The population stress experiment moves held-out observations toward the opposite class. A single common direction is fitted from the downstream-training means and normalized to L1 length one. For $\rho\in\{0,0.25,0.50\}$, class zero is translated by $+\rho v$ and class one by $-\rho v$. Every selector receives the same transformed observations and direction.

\section{Exploratory supplement: retain the whole dual fraction}
\subsection{The experiment-motivated question}
The primary timing audit exposed a weakness of the tangent master: it may repeatedly solve expensive integer models while retaining only a linear approximation to each useful dual fraction. This motivates a supplementary question: can the same certificate constrain future integer supports more effectively if its entire fraction is retained?

The supplement was declared separately after the primary computational observations and before its own runs. It keeps the risk objective, oracle, warm support, root stage, wall limit, and stopping rule. The treatment changes only the master representation, and its outcomes remain exploratory.

\subsection{From a certificate to one linear master row}
Store a repaired conic certificate with positive scale $\tau$, nonnegative prices $\widehat\mu$, and a retained lower numerator $0\le v\le\Hconf(\widehat\Gamma)$. At any support, its valid lower model is
\begin{equation}
f_c(z)=\frac{v}{\tau+\widehat\mu^\top z},\qquad V(z)\ge f_c(z)
\quad\text{for binary }z.
\label{eq:ratio}
\end{equation}
The denominator is positive because $\tau>0$. Dividing the whole mass-and-price certificate by that denominator restores the oracle normalization at the new support. Homogeneity divides its objective by the same amount. This is why the stored fraction remains valid away from its generating support.

Let $U$ be an independently feasible original-test upper bound. Restrict $0\le\eta\le U$ and introduce a \emph{shared} product vector $y_j=\eta z_j$. At binary masks its exact linear representation is
\begin{equation}
0\le y_j\le Uz_j,\qquad y_j\le\eta,\qquad
y_j\ge\eta-U(1-z_j).
\label{eq:products}
\end{equation}
Then $\eta\ge f_c(z)$ is equivalent to the linear row
\begin{equation}
v\le\tau\eta+\widehat\mu^\top y,\qquad
\one^\top y=d\eta.
\label{eq:ratiolinear}
\end{equation}
The budget identity is valid because the search uses full-budget masks. All certificates share the same $D$ product variables; each adds only one ratio row. An original optimal support with $\eta=\OPT$ and $y=\OPT z$ remains feasible, preserving the global lower/upper bound argument.

The master keeps the valid affine tangents as well. The product lift is exact at binary points, but a fractional linear-relaxation point need not satisfy $y=\eta z$. Therefore its projection need not dominate every affine tangent. A concrete counterexample is provided in the supplementary derivation. Retaining both row families prevents that information loss.

For an incumbent $U>0$, the ratio also implies
\begin{equation}
\widehat\mu^\top z\ge v/U-\tau
\quad\text{whenever }V(z)\le U.
\label{eq:exclusion}
\end{equation}
This says a competitive support must collect enough certificate price to reduce the retained overlap below the incumbent. It is an incumbent-dependent necessary condition, not a sufficient condition for a good support. It is already implied by the full ratio lift and bounds, so explicitly adding it is not claimed as a novel strengthening.

The supplementary design uses mean and mixture data, eight selection observations per class, $(D,d)=(8,2),(12,3),(20,3)$, seeds 10--14, five root rounds, no core-point selection, and the same 30-second limit. Each instance pairs the primary tangent master with the ratio-plus-tangent master. Total time includes its larger master and all unsuccessful outcomes.

\section{Reproducibility and conclusions supported by this study}
The core implementation is independent of the historical COPT-based project. It separates data validation, convex oracles and deployment, and the outer global search. Experiment modules provide frozen generators, baselines, metrics, and a runner. Core, logistic, experiment, and plotting dependencies are declared separately in the new environment. Each run preserves its configuration snapshot and digest, selected observed and semantic coordinates, preprocessing parameters, split and permutation seeds, solver bounds and status, elapsed timings, head certificate, stress evaluations, and raw rejection records. Oracle/master traces are separate. A resumed record is accepted only when its configuration digest matches. Third-party source references and data caches are excluded from publication of the independently written repository; source links and retrieval manifests preserve provenance.

The supported theoretical statement is an exact binary objective with a stronger problem-specific convex extension and globally valid lower cuts. The implementation's numerical evidence checks this against independent small transport problems and deployment constraints. The main empirical conclusion must reflect the full result tables: optimization exactness, practical search efficiency, and independent statistical utility can diverge. The experiment sizes are intentionally controlled. The computational design reaches moderate feature dimensions with small source pools, and the statistical design uses small selection samples. Fixed radii and baseline hyperparameters make the comparison reproducible but do not establish optimal tuning. The exploratory ratio master addresses one observed computational limitation and retains its separate status.

Further work should be tied to these findings: improve the master representation or callback implementation if master cost dominates; study objective/radius selection on independent development data if empirical risk and power disagree; and increase selection samples when apparent support choices are unstable. Those are questions raised by the records, not completed claims of this report.

\section{Additional algebra for the reproduced baseline and supplement}
For Wang's paired sample construction, let
$h_{il,j}=k_j(x_i,x_l)+k_j(y_i,y_l)-k_j(x_i,y_l)-k_j(y_i,x_l)$.
The mean and inspected row-based variance coefficients are
\begin{equation}
t=\frac1{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\ne l}h_{il},\qquad
r_i=\frac1n\sum_lh_{il},\qquad
\widehat\Sigma=\frac4n\sum_i(r_i-\bar r)(r_i-\bar r)^\top.
\end{equation}
The paper criterion uses $A=-0.1\widehat\Sigma$ in $t^\top\omega+\omega^\top A\omega$. Independent scalar-kernel fixtures check these quantities and also preserve the inspected source's positive sign. On a fixed support, diagonalize $A$; stationarity gives $x_i=(t_i/2)/(\lambda-d_i)$. Solve the norm equation above the top eigenvalue, with a separate top-eigenspace fill for the hard case. This provides a global weight fit, while support enumeration or labeled approximate search handles sparsity.

For the ratio supplement, write $D_c(z)=\tau+\widehat\mu^\top z$ and $\nu=D_c(z^t)$. The matched tangent of $v/D_c(z)$ is
\begin{equation}
T_c(z)=\frac v\nu-\frac v{\nu^2}\widehat\mu^\top(z-z^t),\qquad
f_c(z)-T_c(z)=\frac{v[D_c(z)-\nu]^2}{D_c(z)\nu^2}\ge0.
\end{equation}
This precisely measures the curvature discarded by one tangent. It is an integer-support information comparison before product relaxation. It does not prove that a fractional product lift dominates the tangent, which is why both rows are retained.

One counterexample uses $f(z)=1/(1+4z_1+5z_2+z_3)$, budget one, anchor $(1,0,0)$, and $U=1/5$. The fractional point $z=(0,5/6,1/6)$, $\eta=1/6$, $y=(0,1/6,0)$ satisfies the product-relaxation inequalities, $\one^\top y=\eta$, and the ratio row at equality. But its $\eta=1/6$ is below the matched tangent $14/75$. The common full-budget identity therefore helps without replacing the affine cuts.
