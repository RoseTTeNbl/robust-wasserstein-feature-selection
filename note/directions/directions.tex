\section{Starting point}\label{sec:start}
The theory note writes the selection problem $\min_{|S|\le d}V(S)$ as
the minimization of a convex function $F$ over $\mathcal Z_d$ and solves
it by outer approximation. In development checks with the hinge loss,
the method evaluated every subset before it closed the gap, whatever
cuts it used, and the continuous relaxation was weak: on an instance
with $D=10$ and $d=2$, $F$ takes the value $0.58$ at the uniform point
$(d/D)\mathbf1$, against the optimum $1.40$.

This note starts from one question: does the weakness belong to the
formulation or to the problem? The answer developed below is that it
belongs to the problem and has a plain meaning. \emph{Mixing
coordinates helps the defender}: a detector that may weigh all
coordinates, or randomize over them, separates the classes better than
any fixed sparse projection. Whether this help is real depends on whom
the defender faces, and the three answers organize the note.
\begin{itemize}
\item Against sampling error the help is spurious. In high dimension a
robust test certifies separation between identical classes; selection
with a radius that grows with the selected dimension removes the effect
(Direction~A, \Secref{sec:honest}).
\item In the optimization the help is the relaxation gap, and it is why
the problem is hard (\Secref{sec:gap}; Direction~B,
\Secref{sec:interdiction}).
\item Against an adversary who must split one transport budget across
coordinates, the help is genuine, and randomized sparse tests collect it
(Direction~C, \Secref{sec:random}).
\end{itemize}
\Secref{sec:thought} records how the argument was found, including the
routes that failed. \Secref{sec:reform} gives the reformulation on which
everything rests. Each direction then lists its reference theory, a
derivation route and its target results, and every statement is marked
as proved, sketched or conjectured. \Secref{sec:plan} sets priorities.

Notation follows the theory note. For a distribution $Q$ on
$\mathbb R^D$, $Q[S]$ is its image under $x\mapsto x[S]$, and every
transport cost uses the $\ell_1$ norm on the coordinates in question.
The hinge loss is $\ell(t)=(1+t)_+$.

\section{Line of thought}\label{sec:thought}
The argument came in five steps, each answering the question that the
previous one raised.

\para{1. What does $V(S)$ measure?} A detector in $\mathcal F_S$ reads
only $x[S]$, and projection maps each Wasserstein ball onto the ball of
the same radius around the projected sample. So $V(S)$ depends only on
the projected samples and the radii. At fixed distributions the smallest
risk over detectors is a classical object: each loss defines an
$f$-divergence~\cite{nguyen2009surrogate,reid2011information}. Hence
$V(S)$ is $2$ minus the smallest divergence the adversary can reach
(\Secref{sec:divergence}). This is Huber's picture of robust
testing~\cite{huber1965robust,huber1973minimax}, with Wasserstein balls
in place of contamination neighbourhoods~\cite{gao2018robust}.

\para{2. What does the adversary do?} For the hinge loss the divergence
is total variation, and reducing it means creating overlap. Overlap is
paid for by moving mass of one class onto mass of the other, so the
adversary solves a partial transport problem, a budgeted relative of
Strassen's coupling theorem~\cite{strassen1965existence}. On samples it
is a bipartite matching (\Secref{sec:matching}).

\para{3. Where does the selection enter?} The matching dual prices
transport at a rate $\lambda$ and leaves a bounded $\lambda$-Lipschitz
witness, so the robust test is a bounded-Lipschitz two-sample statistic
(\Secref{sec:witness}). The selection enters only through the pair
costs, which are linear in $z=\mathbf1_S$. The whole problem is
therefore one compact mixed-integer program, and its relaxation replaces
a set of coordinates by a weighted $\ell_1$ metric (\Secref{sec:gap}).

\para{4. Why is the relaxation weak?} A weighted metric averages
coordinates. Averaging concentrates the cross-class pair costs around
their mean, and the adversary loses the few cheap pairs that it can
always find in a sparse projection. This is the mechanism ``mixing helps
the defender''. The relaxation gap measures it, and NP-hardness shows
that no formulation removes it in the worst case.

\para{5. Is the help real?} It depends on the adversary, which gives the
three directions of \Secref{sec:start}.

\para{Routes that failed.}
(a)~Perspective strengthening, which makes sparse regression
easy~\cite{bertsimas2021unified}, relies on a strongly convex ridge
term. Here $z$ enters only through the linear cost
$\sum_mz_m|x[m]-y[m]|$, whose perspective is itself, and the relaxation
is already the weighted-metric problem.
(b)~Bounding each pair cost by its $d$ largest coordinate differences
gives a valid bound, but each pair then uses its own coordinates, and
the bound is never stronger than the relaxation (\Secref{sec:gap}).
(c)~The randomized value of Direction~C is a lower bound for the fixed
problem, but on every instance of \Tabref{tab:random} it lies below the
relaxation.
(d)~Cut engineering for outer approximation, such as least favorable
cuts, Pareto cuts or several tangents per evaluation, reduced oracle
time by $30$--$45\%$ in development checks, but cannot close a gap that
belongs to the problem.
(e)~Sinkhorn ambiguity sets place the selection inside
$\exp(-c/\varepsilon)$ and lose the matching structure; neural
generators of least favorable distributions lose the certificate.

\section{The reformulation}\label{sec:reform}
\subsection{The robust value is a divergence}\label{sec:divergence}
For $a,b\ge0$ let $\psi(a,b)=\inf_{t\in\mathbb R}\{a\ell(-t)+b\ell(t)\}$.
It is concave and positively homogeneous, and $\psi(a,a)=2a$ because
convexity gives $\ell(-t)+\ell(t)\ge2\ell(0)=2$. Hence
\[
D_\ell(Q_0,Q_1)=2-\int\psi(dQ_0,dQ_1)
\]
is an $f$-divergence between probability distributions. For
$\ell(t)=(1+t)_+$, $e^t$ and $\log_2(1+e^t)$ it equals $2\,\TV$, the
squared Hellinger distance $\int(\sqrt{dQ_0}-\sqrt{dQ_1})^2$, and twice
the Jensen--Shannon divergence in bits. Write
$\mathcal P_k[S]=\{Q:\mathcal W_1(\widehat{\mathbb P}_k[S],Q)\le\rho_k\}$.

\begin{proposition}[Divergence form; proved]\label{prop:divergence}
For every $S\subseteq[D]$,
$V(S)=2-\min_{Q_k\in\mathcal P_k[S]}D_\ell(Q_0,Q_1)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Fix $Q_0$ and $Q_1$. Minimizing the risk
$\mathbb E_{Q_0}\ell(-f)+\mathbb E_{Q_1}\ell(f)$ point by point over
$f\in\mathcal F_S$ gives $\int\psi(dQ_0[S],dQ_1[S])$. Projection maps the
ambiguity sets into $\mathcal P_k[S]$, and every element of
$\mathcal P_k[S]$ lifts with the same cost. Exchanging the minimum over
$f$ and the supremum over distributions therefore gives
$V(S)\ge2-\inf D_\ell$, and Lemma~2.1 of the theory note attains this
bound with distributions supported on the projected samples.
\end{proof}

\para{Reference theory.} The correspondence between losses and
divergences is due to Nguyen, Wainwright and
Jordan~\cite{nguyen2009surrogate} and is developed by Reid and
Williamson~\cite{reid2011information}. Least favorable pairs for robust
tests go back to Huber~\cite{huber1965robust} and Huber and
Strassen~\cite{huber1973minimax}; Wasserstein balls are those
of~\cite{gao2018robust}. Selecting a projection that maximizes a
distance between two samples is the theme of principal differences
analysis~\cite{mueller2015principal}, projected Wasserstein
tests~\cite{wang2021projected}, subspace robust
distances~\cite{paty2019subspace}, feature-robust
transport~\cite{petrovich2022feature} and kernel variable
selection~\cite{wang2023variable}. Here the distance is a divergence
measured after a transport attack, and its value is the risk certificate
of a minimax test. The mirror construction, a Wasserstein distance
measured after a total-variation attack, is the robust transport
of~\cite{nietert2023robust}.

\subsection{Hinge loss: a budgeted matching}\label{sec:matching}
For the hinge loss the divergence is total variation, and the attack
becomes combinatorial. Write $C^m_{ij}=|x_i^{(0)}[m]-x_j^{(1)}[m]|$ and
$c_{ij}(S)=\sum_{m\in S}C^m_{ij}$, and let
\begin{equation}\label{eq:matching}
m_S(\rho)=\max_{\pi\ge0}\Bigl\{\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}:\
\sum_j\pi_{ij}\le\frac1{n_0},\ \sum_i\pi_{ij}\le\frac1{n_1},\
\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}c_{ij}(S)\le\rho\Bigr\}
\end{equation}
be the largest cross-class mass that can be paired within a total
transport budget $\rho$.

\begin{proposition}[Matching form; proved]\label{prop:matching}
For the hinge loss, $V(S)=2\,m_S(\rho_0+\rho_1)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By \Nref{Proposition}{prop:divergence}, $V(S)/2=1-\min\TV(Q_0,Q_1)$ is
the largest overlap $\int\min(dQ_0,dQ_1)$ of two distributions in the
projected balls. Let transport plans $\gamma_0,\gamma_1$ reach $Q_0,Q_1$
from the projected samples. At each point, split the overlap by the
source of its class-$0$ mass and, independently, by the source of its
class-$1$ mass. This gives masses $\pi_{ij}$ with row sums at most
$1/n_0$, column sums at most $1/n_1$, and total equal to the overlap. A
unit of $\pi_{ij}$ reaches a common point from $x_i^{(0)}[S]$ and from
$x_j^{(1)}[S]$, so by the triangle inequality it costs the two plans
together at least $c_{ij}(S)$. Hence the overlap is at most
$m_S(\rho_0+\rho_1)$. Conversely, let $\pi$ be feasible with
$\rho=\rho_0+\rho_1$, set $\theta=\rho_0/\rho$, and send mass $\pi_{ij}$
of each class to the point
$x_i^{(0)}[S]+\theta(x_j^{(1)}[S]-x_i^{(0)}[S])$. Class~$0$ pays
$\theta c_{ij}(S)$ per unit and class~$1$ pays $(1-\theta)c_{ij}(S)$, so
both budgets hold, and the overlap is at least $\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}$.
\end{proof}

Three consequences are used below. First, the radii enter only through
$\rho_0+\rho_1$; on random instances with unequal radii the solver of
the theory note agrees with $2m_S$ to $10^{-10}$. Second, the least
favorable distributions pair up cross-class points: the adversary is a
matchmaker with a transport budget, and $S$ separates the classes when
few cross-class pairs are cheap in the coordinates of $S$. Third,
evaluating $V(S)$ is a bipartite matching with one side constraint and
$n_0n_1$ variables, instead of a transport plan over $N^2$ pairs with
two budgets.

The proof never uses that the distributions are empirical. For any
$P_0,P_1$, the largest overlap reachable within budgets $r_0,r_1$ is the
largest mass of a partial coupling of $P_0[S]$ and $P_1[S]$ whose
transport cost is at most $r_0+r_1$. This is the budget form of optimal
partial transport~\cite{figalli2010optimal,caffarelli2010free,chapel2020partial},
and it plays for Wasserstein balls the role that Strassen's
theorem~\cite{strassen1965existence} plays for Prokhorov
neighbourhoods: it turns a statement about perturbed distributions into
one about couplings.

\subsection{The dual: bounded Lipschitz witnesses}\label{sec:witness}
For $\lambda\ge0$ and distributions $Q_0,Q_1$ on $\mathbb R^{|S|}$,
let
\[
\beta_\lambda(Q_0,Q_1)=\sup\bigl\{\mathbb E_{Q_0}h-\mathbb E_{Q_1}h:\
|h|\le\tfrac12,\ |h(\xi)-h(\xi')|\le\lambda\|\xi-\xi'\|_1\bigr\}.
\]
This is a variant of Dudley's bounded-Lipschitz
distance~\cite{dudley2002real} with the Lipschitz constant set to
$\lambda$. It increases from $0$ at
$\lambda=0$ to $\TV(Q_0,Q_1)$ as $\lambda\to\infty$, and it is concave
in $\lambda$, since $th_1+(1-t)h_2$ is admissible for
$t\lambda_1+(1-t)\lambda_2$.

\begin{proposition}[Lipschitz witness; proved]\label{prop:witness}
For the hinge loss,
\[
V(S)=2-2\max_{\lambda\ge0}\bigl\{\beta_\lambda\bigl(\widehat{\mathbb P}_0[S],
\widehat{\mathbb P}_1[S]\bigr)-\lambda(\rho_0+\rho_1)\bigr\},
\]
and if $(\lambda,h)$ attains the maximum, the detector $f(x)=2h(x[S])$
is optimal: $R(f)=V(S)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Write $\rho=\rho_0+\rho_1$. Dualizing only the budget in
\Eqref{eq:matching} gives $m_S(\rho)=\min_{\lambda\ge0}\{\lambda\rho+M(\lambda)\}$,
where $M(\lambda)=\max_\pi\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}(1-\lambda c_{ij}(S))$ over
the row and column constraints. The dual of $M(\lambda)$ is
$\min\{\bar u+\bar v:\ u_i+v_j\ge1-\lambda c_{ij}(S),\ u,v\ge0\}$, with
$\bar u=\frac1{n_0}\sum_iu_i$ and $\bar v=\frac1{n_1}\sum_jv_j$. Put
$h_i=\frac12-u_i$ at the class-$0$ points and $h'_j=v_j-\frac12$ at the
class-$1$ points. Then $1-M(\lambda)$ is the largest value of
$\frac1{n_0}\sum_ih_i-\frac1{n_1}\sum_jh'_j$ subject to
$h_i-h'_j\le\lambda c_{ij}(S)$, $h_i\le\frac12$ and $h'_j\ge-\frac12$.
Raising any $h_i<-\frac12$ to $-\frac12$, or lowering any
$h'_j>\frac12$ to $\frac12$, keeps feasibility and does not decrease the
objective. The clipped function
$h(\xi)=\max\{-\frac12,\min\{\frac12,\min_j(h'_j+\lambda\|x_j^{(1)}[S]-\xi\|_1)\}\}$
is then admissible in $\beta_\lambda$, is at least $h_i$ at the class-$0$
points and at most $h'_j$ at the class-$1$ points. Conversely every
admissible $h$ gives feasible values, so $1-M(\lambda)=\beta_\lambda$,
and \Nref{Proposition}{prop:matching} gives the formula. For the detector,
$|2h|\le1$ makes the hinge losses linear, and Kantorovich--Rubinstein
duality gives, for every admissible pair $(Q_0,Q_1)$,
\[
\mathbb E_{Q_0}(1-2h)+\mathbb E_{Q_1}(1+2h)
\le2-2\bigl(\mathbb E_{\widehat{\mathbb P}_0}h-\mathbb E_{\widehat{\mathbb P}_1}h\bigr)
+2\lambda\rho
=2-2\bigl(\beta_\lambda-\lambda\rho\bigr)=V(S).\qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

On random instances the formula agrees with \Eqref{eq:matching} to
$10^{-8}$. The proposition gives the robust hinge test a plain reading:
it is a bounded-Lipschitz two-sample statistic whose Lipschitz constant
is priced at the total radius. A large $\lambda$ lets the witness
separate the samples but pays $\lambda\rho$; the radius decides how
smooth an honest witness must be. Variable selection chooses the $d$
coordinates on which this penalized distance is largest, the analogue of
selecting coordinates for a kernel two-sample test~\cite{wang2023variable}.
The function $1-M(\lambda)$ is also an unbalanced transport cost in
which unmatched mass is paid at a fixed price, the generalized
Wasserstein distance of~\cite{piccoli2014generalized}, whose dual is the
bounded-Lipschitz norm~\cite{hanin1992kantorovich}. Finally, $m_S$ is
the minimum of the tangents $\rho\mapsto\lambda\rho+M(\lambda)$, so it
is concave and nondecreasing in $\rho$ with slope equal to the optimal
price; \Secref{sec:random} uses these tangents.

\section{Relaxation and hardness}\label{sec:gap}
Linear programming duality for \Eqref{eq:matching} gives
\[
m_S(\rho)=\min_{u,v,\lambda\ge0}\bigl\{\rho\lambda+\bar u+\bar v:\
u_i+v_j+\lambda c_{ij}(S)\ge1\bigr\}.
\]
Each cross-class pair is either paid for by its endpoints or separated
by its priced distance $\lambda c_{ij}(S)$, and selecting $S$ decides
which coordinates the adversary pays for. With $w_m=\lambda z_m$ and
$\rho=\rho_0+\rho_1$, the whole selection problem becomes one compact
program:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:milp}
\begin{aligned}
\min_{z\in\mathcal Z_d,\,u,v,w,\lambda\ge0}\quad&
\rho\lambda+\bar u+\bar v\\
\text{subject to}\quad&
u_i+v_j+\sum_m w_mC^m_{ij}\ge1,\qquad
w_m\le\lambda,\qquad \rho w_m\le z_m,\qquad
\sum_m w_m\le d\lambda.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Compact program; proved]\label{prop:milp}
The optimal value of \Eqref{eq:milp} is
$\min_{|S|\le d}m_S(\rho)=\frac12\min_{|S|\le d}V(S)$. Its continuous
relaxation, with $\mathcal Z_d$ replaced by
$\{z\in[0,1]^D:\mathbf1^\top z\le d\}$, has the value
$\min_\zeta m_\zeta(\rho)$ over the same set, where $m_\zeta$ is
$m_S$ with the cost $c_{ij}(S)$ replaced by the weighted cost
$\sum_m\zeta_mC^m_{ij}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The point $u=\mathbf1$, $v=0$, $\lambda=0$ has value $1$, so
$\rho\lambda\le1$ at an optimum. For $z=\mathbf1_S$ the constraints
force $w_m=0$ outside $S$ and allow $w_m=\lambda$ inside, which is
optimal because $C^m_{ij}\ge0$; the value is the dual of $m_S(\rho)$.
For the relaxation, a feasible point with $\lambda>0$ gives
$\zeta=w/\lambda$ in the relaxed set and the dual objective of
$m_\zeta(\rho)$; with $\lambda=0$ the rows give $\bar u+\bar v\ge1$.
Conversely, for $\zeta$ in the relaxed set and an optimal dual
$(u,v,\lambda)$ of $m_\zeta(\rho)$, the point $z=\zeta$, $w=\lambda\zeta$
is feasible because $\rho\lambda\le1$.
\end{proof}

The relaxation lets the defender choose a weighted $\ell_1$ metric
instead of a set of coordinates, and weighted metrics are genuinely
better defenses. Averaging coordinates concentrates the pair costs
$\sum_m\zeta_mC^m_{ij}$ around their mean, and the adversary loses the
cheap pairs it finds in a sparse projection. \Tabref{tab:gap} shows the
effect: the relaxed value is one half to two thirds of the optimum. It
is still stronger than the relaxation of the theory note, whose $F$
already takes the value $0.58$ at the uniform point on the first
instance. Since every relaxed cost satisfies
$\sum_m\zeta_mC^m_{ij}\le T_{ij}$, the sum of the $d$ largest
$C^m_{ij}$, and $m$ decreases with the costs, the pairwise bound
$m_T(\rho)$ of \Secref{sec:thought}(b) never exceeds the relaxation.

The combinatorial core is also hard in the worst case.

\begin{proposition}[Hardness; proved]\label{prop:hard}
Let the data be binary and $\rho_0+\rho_1<\min\{1/n_0,1/n_1\}$. Then
$\min_{|S|\le d}V(S)\le2(\rho_0+\rho_1)$ if and only if some $S$ with
$|S|\le d$ separates every cross-class pair, that is,
$x_i^{(0)}[S]\ne x_j^{(1)}[S]$ for all $i,j$. Deciding this is the
$k$-feature-set problem, which is NP-complete~\cite{davies1994np} and
W[2]-complete in $d$~\cite{cotta2003kfeature}. Hence computing
$\min_{|S|\le d}V(S)$ is NP-hard.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Write $\rho=\rho_0+\rho_1$. If $S$ separates every pair, then
$c_{ij}(S)\ge1$ for all pairs and $m_S(\rho)\le\rho$. Otherwise some
pair has $c_{ij}(S)=0$ and can be matched at no cost, so
$m_S(\rho)\ge\min\{1/n_0,1/n_1\}>\rho$.
\Nref{Proposition}{prop:matching} completes the proof.
\end{proof}

Diminishing returns do not rescue greedy selection either. With class
$0$ at $(0,0),(1,1)$ and class $1$ at $(0,1),(1,0)$, each coordinate
alone leaves the projected classes identical, so $V=2$, while the two
coordinates together give $V=2(\rho_0+\rho_1)$ when
$\rho_0+\rho_1\le1$. A strong convex relaxation is therefore not to be
expected, and progress must use information that the weighted-metric
problem does not see: the sample size (Direction~A), the matching
structure (Direction~B), or the value of mixing itself (Direction~C).

\section{Direction A: selection makes robust tests honest}\label{sec:honest}
\subsection{The phenomenon}
\Tabref{tab:noise} draws both classes from the same distribution, so
the correct robust value is $2$. Yet $V([D])$ falls to $0.03$ at
$D=50$. In high dimension the empirical points are isolated, every
cross-class pair is expensive to match, and the robust test certifies a
separation that does not exist. Selection limits the effect, but the
best of many subsets is optimistic too, and in the first instance of
\Tabref{tab:gap} the optimal pair consists of two uninformative
coordinates. A radius that ignores the dimension is not honest.

\para{Reference theory.} Rates of empirical measures in
$\mathcal W_1$~\cite{fournier2015rate,weed2019sharp} and the
bounded-difference inequality~\cite{mcdiarmid1989method} give coverage;
the estimation of integral probability
metrics~\cite{sriperumbudur2012empirical} is the same question for the
witness class of \Nref{Proposition}{prop:witness}. Finite-sample
Wasserstein robust optimization~\cite{gao2023finite} shows when smaller
radii suffice. Transport between distributions that differ only in a
few directions is the spiked transport
model~\cite{nilesweed2022spiked}, close to subspace
methods~\cite{paty2019subspace,wang2021projected}. Choosing a model size
by a data-dependent bound is structural risk
minimization~\cite{bartlett2002model}.

\para{Route.} Two deterministic facts suffice. The triangle inequality
nests the empirical and population balls, which sandwiches $V(S)$
between population values (\Nref{Lemma}{lem:sandwich}); and the detector
certificate degrades linearly in the coverage error
(\Nref{Proposition}{prop:honest}). Probability enters once, through the
event that every projected empirical distribution of dimension at most
$d$ lies within $\varepsilon$ of its population. Its radius follows from
a dyadic $\mathcal W_1$ bound in dimension $d$, a bounded-difference
inequality and a union bound over the $\binom Dd$ subsets
(\Nref{Lemma}{lem:coverage}). Nearest-neighbour distances give a matching
lower bound (\Nref{Proposition}{prop:collapse}).

\para{Population objects.} Let $\mathbb P_0,\mathbb P_1$ be the true
distributions, $\Rt(f)=\mathbb E_{\mathbb P_0}\ell(-f)+\mathbb E_{\mathbb P_1}\ell(f)$,
and, for radii $r=(r_0,r_1)$,
\[
\Vp(S;r)=2-\inf\bigl\{D_\ell(Q_0,Q_1):\
\mathcal W_1(\mathbb P_k[S],Q_k)\le r_k,\ k=0,1\bigr\},\qquad
B_S=\Vp(S;0).
\]
By the proof of \Nref{Proposition}{prop:divergence},
$B_S=\min_{f\in\mathcal F_S}\Rt(f)$ is the Bayes risk of the loss from
the coordinates $S$; for the hinge loss
$B_S=2(1-\TV(\mathbb P_0[S],\mathbb P_1[S]))$ is twice the smallest
total error of a test that reads $S$. $\Vp$ increases with $r$, and
$s\mapsto\Vp(S;sr)$ is concave, because $D_\ell$ is jointly convex and
the balls grow linearly in $s$. We write $V(S;\rho)$ for the empirical
value with radii $\rho=(\rho_0,\rho_1)$.

\subsection{Results}
\begin{lemma}[Sandwich; proved]\label{lem:sandwich}
If $\mathcal W_1(\widehat{\mathbb P}_k[S],\mathbb P_k[S])\le\varepsilon_k\le\rho_k$
for $k=0,1$, then
\[
B_S\le\Vp(S;\rho-\varepsilon)\le V(S;\rho)\le\Vp(S;\rho+\varepsilon).
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
By the triangle inequality, the ball of radius $\rho_k-\varepsilon_k$
around $\mathbb P_k[S]$ lies in the ball of radius $\rho_k$ around
$\widehat{\mathbb P}_k[S]$, which lies in the ball of radius
$\rho_k+\varepsilon_k$ around $\mathbb P_k[S]$. By
\Nref{Proposition}{prop:divergence} the robust value increases with the
balls.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Certificate of the detector; proved]\label{prop:honest}
Let $(g,a,\lambda)$ solve the detector program of the theory note at $S$,
and let $f$ be its extension in Appendix~A there, used as the test that
declares class~$1$ when $f<0$. For any $\mathbb P_0,\mathbb P_1$ with
finite first moments,
\[
\mathbb P_0(f<0)+\mathbb P_1(f\ge0)\le\Rt(f)
\le V(S)+\sum_{k=0}^1\lambda_k\bigl(\mathcal W_1(\widehat{\mathbb P}_k[S],
\mathbb P_k[S])-\rho_k\bigr).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The first inequality holds because $\ell\ge1$ on $[0,\infty)$. The
envelope $E_k$ of Appendix~A dominates the class-$k$ loss of $f$, is
$\lambda_k$-Lipschitz on $\mathbb R^{|S|}$, and satisfies
$E_k(x_i^{(k)}[S])\le a_i^{(k)}$. Kantorovich--Rubinstein duality gives
$\mathbb E_{\mathbb P_k}E_k\le\frac1{n_k}\sum_ia_i^{(k)}
+\lambda_k\mathcal W_1(\widehat{\mathbb P}_k[S],\mathbb P_k[S])$.
Summing over $k$ gives the second inequality.
\end{proof}

The next result shows that the radius must grow with the selected
dimension.

\begin{proposition}[Radius lower bound; proved]\label{prop:collapse}
Use the hinge loss, let $\mathbb P_0=\mathbb P_1$, and let the
projection on $S$, $|S|=d$, have a density bounded by $M$. With
probability at least $1-e^{-n_0/8}$, for every $c\in(0,1)$,
\[
V(S)\ge2-c\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\rho_0+\rho_1\ge(1-c)\,\frac d{4e}\,(4Mn_1)^{-1/d}.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $\delta_i$ be the $\ell_1$ distance from $x_i^{(0)}[S]$ to the
nearest projected class-$1$ point. Given class~$1$, the $\delta_i$ are
independent, and $\Pr(\delta_i<r)\le n_1M(2r)^d/d!$, the volume of
$n_1$ $\ell_1$ balls times $M$. Let $r^\star$ make this bound equal to
$1/4$; $d!\ge(d/e)^d$ gives $r^\star\ge\frac d{2e}(4Mn_1)^{-1/d}$. By
Hoeffding's inequality, with probability at least $1-e^{-n_0/8}$ at
least half of the $\delta_i$ are at least $r^\star$. If $V(S)\ge2-c$,
then $m_S\ge1-c/2$ by \Nref{Proposition}{prop:matching}, so at most $c/2$
of the class-$0$ mass is unmatched. The far points carry mass at least
$1/2$, hence contribute matched mass at least $(1-c)/2$, each unit
costing at least $r^\star$. So $\rho_0+\rho_1\ge(1-c)r^\star/2$.
\end{proof}

For \Tabref{tab:noise} with $D=10$, where $M=(2\pi)^{-5}$ and
$n_1=15$, the bound forbids $V([D])\ge1.5$ at $\rho_0+\rho_1=0.6$ with
probability at least $0.84$, consistent with the observed mean $0.21$. The order
$d\,n^{-1/d}$ is also sufficient.

\begin{lemma}[Uniform coverage; standard, sketched]\label{lem:coverage}
Let $\mathbb P_0,\mathbb P_1$ be supported in $[0,1]^D$, $3\le d\le D$
and $\delta\in(0,1)$. With probability at least $1-\delta$, for $k=0,1$
and all $|S|\le d$,
\[
\mathcal W_1(\widehat{\mathbb P}_k[S],\mathbb P_k[S])\le\varepsilon_k
:=c\,d\,n_k^{-1/d}+d\sqrt{\frac{d\log(eD/d)+\log(2/\delta)}{2n_k}},
\]
with an absolute constant $c$; the argument below gives $c\le9$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{sketch}
Projecting onto a subset contracts $\mathcal W_1$, so it suffices to
take $|S|=d$. (i)~Expectation. Partition $[0,1]^d$ into the $2^{jd}$
dyadic cubes of side $2^{-j}$, whose $\ell_1$ diameter is $d2^{-j}$. The
dyadic bound of~\cite[Proposition~1]{weed2019sharp} gives
$\mathcal W_1\le d\bigl(2^{-J}+\sum_{j=1}^J2^{1-j}\sum_Q|\widehat{\mathbb P}(Q)-\mathbb P(Q)|\bigr)$.
Since $\mathbb E|\widehat{\mathbb P}(Q)-\mathbb P(Q)|\le\sqrt{\mathbb P(Q)/n}$
and Cauchy--Schwarz gives $\sum_Q\sqrt{\mathbb P(Q)}\le2^{jd/2}$, the
choice $2^J\le n^{1/d}<2^{J+1}$ yields
$\mathbb E\mathcal W_1\le9\,d\,n^{-1/d}$ for $d\ge3$. (ii)~Concentration.
Replacing one sample moves mass $1/n$ by at most $d$, so McDiarmid's
inequality bounds the deviation by $d\sqrt{\log(1/\delta')/(2n)}$ with
probability $1-\delta'$. (iii)~A union bound over two classes and
$\binom Dd\le(eD/d)^d$ subsets. For $d=1,2$ the first term becomes of
order $n^{-1/2}$ and $n^{-1/2}\log n$~\cite{fournier2015rate}.
\end{sketch}

\begin{theorem}[Honest selection; proved given Lemma~\ref{lem:coverage}]\label{thm:honest}
Let $\varepsilon$ be as in \Nref{Lemma}{lem:coverage} and
$\rho_k\ge\varepsilon_k$. With probability at least $1-\delta$,
simultaneously for all $|S|\le d$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] $B_S\le V(S;\rho)\le\Vp(S;2\rho)$;
\item[(b)] the detector $f_S$ of the theory note at $S$ satisfies
$\mathbb P_0(f_S<0)+\mathbb P_1(f_S\ge0)\le\Rt(f_S)\le V(S;\rho)$.
\end{enumerate}
In particular the selected $\widehat S$ and its detector $\widehat f$
satisfy
\[
\mathbb P_0(\widehat f<0)+\mathbb P_1(\widehat f\ge0)\le\Rt(\widehat f)
\le V(\widehat S;\rho)\le\min_{|S|\le d}\Vp(S;2\rho),
\]
and a selection that is optimal only up to $\tau$ satisfies the same
chain with $+\tau$ at the right end.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
On the event of \Nref{Lemma}{lem:coverage}, \Nref{Lemma}{lem:sandwich}
gives (a) because $\rho+\varepsilon\le2\rho$, and
\Nref{Proposition}{prop:honest} gives (b) because
$\varepsilon_k\le\rho_k$. The chain follows from
$V(\widehat S;\rho)\le V(S;\rho)$ for all $|S|\le d$.
\end{proof}

The theorem says three things. The certificate stays valid after
selection with no correction, because the event covers all subsets at
once; and since validity holds for every $S$, exact optimization buys
only the right end of the chain. The certificate never claims more
separation than the Bayes risk allows, which removes the collapse of
\Tabref{tab:noise}. And it is accurate up to doubling the radius. By
\Nref{Proposition}{prop:collapse}, the order $d\,n^{-1/d}$ of the radius
cannot be lowered for this detector class.

\begin{corollary}[Budget chosen by the certificate; proved given Lemma~\ref{lem:coverage}]\label{cor:budget}
For each $d\le d_{\max}$ let $\rho(d)$ be the radius of
\Nref{Lemma}{lem:coverage} at level $\delta/d_{\max}$ (or its analogue for
$d\le2$), and let $(\widehat d,\widehat S)$ minimize $V(S;\rho(d))$ over
$d\le d_{\max}$ and $|S|\le d$. With probability at least $1-\delta$,
\[
\Rt(\widehat f)\le V(\widehat S;\rho(\widehat d))
\le\min_{d\le d_{\max}}\ \min_{|S|\le d}\Vp(S;2\rho(d)).
\]
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Apply \Nref{Theorem}{thm:honest} at each $d$ and take a union bound.
\end{proof}

The radius plays the role of the complexity penalty in structural risk
minimization~\cite{bartlett2002model}: more coordinates separate the
classes better but pay a larger radius, and the certificate balances the
two without a separate penalty.

\subsection{Rates and recovery}
The right end of \Nref{Theorem}{thm:honest} is the Bayes risk plus the
modulus $\Vp(S;2\rho)-B_S$. Two hinge-loss examples fix its order.
If $\mathbb P_0[S]$ and $\mathbb P_1[S]$ are supported on sets at
$\ell_1$ distance at least $\Delta$, every unit of matched mass costs at
least $\Delta$, so $\Vp(S;r)\le2(r_0+r_1)/\Delta$ and $B_S=0$. If
$\mathbb P_0=U[0,1]$ and $\mathbb P_1=U[1,2]$ on one coordinate, the
cheapest way to match mass $m$ pairs $[1-m,1]$ with $[1,1+m]$ at cost
$m^2$, so $\Vp(S;r)=2\sqrt{r_0+r_1}$ for $r_0+r_1\le1$; smooth densities
that cross behave alike, heuristically. With $\rho_k=\varepsilon_k$ and
$s=|S^\star|$, the certified error is thus of order $s\,n^{-1/s}/\Delta$
in the first case and $(s\,n^{-1/s})^{1/2}$ in the second, up to the
$\log(eD/s)$ term. The ambient dimension enters only through that
logarithm, whereas $S=[D]$ would need a radius of order $D\,n^{-1/D}$.

\begin{proposition}[Recovery; proved given Lemma~\ref{lem:coverage}]\label{prop:recovery}
Let $|S^\star|=s$ and suppose that
$\Vp(S;\rho)\ge\Vp(S^\star;\rho)+\gamma$ for all $S\ne S^\star$ with
$|S|=s$. If the radii of \Nref{Lemma}{lem:coverage} at level $s$ satisfy
$\varepsilon_k\le\eta\rho_k$ with $4\eta/(1-\eta)<\gamma$, then with
probability at least $1-\delta$ the selection at budget $s$ returns
$S^\star$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The map $t\mapsto\Vp(S;t\rho)$ is concave, nondecreasing and takes
values in $[0,2]$, so its slope on $[1-\eta,1+\eta]$ is at most
$2/(1-\eta)$. On the coverage event, \Nref{Lemma}{lem:sandwich} gives
$V(S;\rho)\ge\Vp(S;(1-\eta)\rho)\ge\Vp(S;\rho)-2\eta/(1-\eta)$ for
every $S$, and
$V(S^\star;\rho)\le\Vp(S^\star;(1+\eta)\rho)\le\Vp(S^\star;\rho)+2\eta/(1-\eta)$.
Hence $V(S;\rho)>V(S^\star;\rho)$ for every $S\ne S^\star$.
\end{proof}

Recovery thus needs $n_k$ of order
$(Cs/\eta\rho)^s+s^3\log(eD/s)/(\eta\rho)^2$, with an absolute constant
$C$ and $\rho=\min_k\rho_k$: exponential in the selected dimension,
logarithmic in the ambient one, as in the power
analyses of projected tests~\cite{wang2021projected,wang2023variable}.

\subsection{Open problems}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Sharp radius.} The radius lies between
$\frac d{4e}(4Mn)^{-1/d}$ (\Nref{Proposition}{prop:collapse}) and
$c\,d\,n^{-1/d}+d^{3/2}\sqrt{\log(eD/d)/n}$ (\Nref{Lemma}{lem:coverage}).
Is the $\log(eD/d)$ term necessary? A lower bound would study the
minimum of $V(S)$ over all $\binom Dd$ subsets for identical classes,
whose optimism the second row of \Tabref{tab:noise} shows.
\item \emph{Restricted detectors.} The order $n^{-1/d}$ is the price of
the nonparametric class $\mathcal F_S$: the adversary of
\Nref{Proposition}{prop:collapse} defeats detectors that memorize isolated
points. For linear or kernel detectors the variation analysis
of~\cite{gao2023finite} suggests radii of order
$\sqrt{d\log(eD/d)/n}$, at the cost of the finite program of Lemma~2.1.
The target is a version of \Nref{Theorem}{thm:honest} with such radii.
\item \emph{Honest relaxation.} A weighted metric with weights
$\zeta_m=d/D$ has, at scales below $d/D$, the covering numbers of a
$D$-dimensional cube, so its honest radius is of $D$-dimensional order.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{conjecture}[Honest relaxation]\label{conj:relax}
If each weighted metric in \Nref{Proposition}{prop:milp} is given the
radius that makes it honest, the relaxation no longer improves on the
best sparse selection by more than the width of the sandwich in
\Nref{Lemma}{lem:sandwich}. The gap of \Secref{sec:gap} would then be a
statistical artifact.
\end{conjecture}

\noindent The first experiments are \Tabref{tab:noise} with the radii of
\Nref{Lemma}{lem:coverage}, recovery frequencies against $n$ for
$D\in\{20,100\}$, and a comparison with kernel
selection~\cite{wang2023variable}.

\section{Direction B: selection as matching interdiction}\label{sec:interdiction}
\para{Reference theory.} Interdiction games and bilevel integer
programming~\cite{smith2020survey}, matching
interdiction~\cite{zenklusen2010matching}, interdiction with monotone
followers~\cite{fischetti2019interdiction}, outer
approximation~\cite{bertsimas2021unified}, and the hardness of feature
sets~\cite{davies1994np,cotta2003kfeature}.

\para{Route.} Program \Eqref{eq:milp} is exact, and
\Nref{Proposition}{prop:hard} says that a search tree cannot be avoided.
The remaining gains must therefore come from the structure of the
follower, which is a matching, and from a statistical reason to stop
early.

\para{The game.} By \Secref{sec:gap},
$\min_{|S|\le d}m_S(\rho)$ is a two-player game: the defender raises
pair costs by choosing coordinates, and the adversary buys the largest
cross-class matching it can afford. Removing arcs instead of pricing
coordinates is matching interdiction~\cite{zenklusen2010matching}. The
adversary's feasible set shrinks as coordinates are added, the monotone
setting of~\cite{fischetti2019interdiction}. \Tabref{tab:gap} shows the
limit of a generic solver: the tree grows almost as fast as
enumeration, as \Secref{sec:gap} predicts.

\begin{proposition}[Matching bounds; proved]\label{prop:bounds}
Let $\pi\ge0$ satisfy the row and column constraints of
\Eqref{eq:matching}, and let $\kappa_m=\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}C^m_{ij}$. For
every $S$,
\[
m_S(\rho)\ge\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\cdot\min\Bigl\{1,
\frac\rho{\sum_{m\in S}\kappa_m}\Bigr\}.
\]
At a node of a search tree that fixes the coordinates $I$ in and leaves
$J$ free, every completion satisfies the bound with
$\sum_{m\in S}\kappa_m$ replaced by $\sum_{m\in I}\kappa_m$ plus the sum
of the $d-|I|$ largest $\kappa_m$, $m\in J$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Scaling $\pi$ by the minimum keeps the row and column constraints and
brings its cost under $S$ within $\rho$. The node bound follows because
the bound decreases in $\sum_{m\in S}\kappa_m$.
\end{proof}

The bound charges a whole matching its exact cost under $S$, while the
cuts of the theory note are affine in $z$. It costs $O(D\log D)$ per
matching and node, and a pool of matchings collected from evaluated
subsets gives the maximum of such bounds. Its strength is untested.

\begin{corollary}[Honest tolerance; proved given Lemma~\ref{lem:coverage}]\label{cor:tolerance}
If the search stops at absolute gap $\tau$ with the incumbent
$\widehat S_\tau$, then on the event of \Nref{Theorem}{thm:honest},
$\Rt(\widehat f)\le V(\widehat S_\tau;\rho)\le\min_{|S|\le d}\Vp(S;2\rho)+\tau$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Part~(b) of \Nref{Theorem}{thm:honest} holds for every $S$, and
$V(\widehat S_\tau;\rho)\le\min_SV(S;\rho)+\tau$.
\end{proof}

A natural $\tau$ is the width of the sandwich: with
$\varepsilon_k\le\eta\rho_k$, the proof of
\Nref{Proposition}{prop:recovery} confines each $V(S;\rho)$ to an
interval of length $4\eta/(1-\eta)$ around $\Vp(S;\rho)$, so smaller
differences carry no statistical meaning. Tolerance alone does not save
much, however. On the $D=50$ instance of \Tabref{tab:gap}, relative
tolerances of $10\%$, $5\%$ and $2\%$ stopped after $209$, $211$ and
$220$\,s, against $235$\,s at the default tolerance, each run with the
optimal subset; the solver finds the optimum early and spends its time
raising the lower bound. These are single development runs.

\begin{proposition}[General losses; sketched]\label{prop:general}
For any loss in the theory note, the problem
\[
\begin{aligned}
\min\quad&\sum_{k=0}^1\Bigl(\frac1{n_k}\sum_ia_i^{(k)}+\rho_k\lambda_k\Bigr)\\
\text{subject to}\quad&
\ell((-1)^{k+1}g_y)\le a_i^{(k)}+\sum_mw_{km}|x_i^{(k)}[m]-y[m]|,\\
&0\le w_{km}\le\lambda_k,\qquad \rho_kw_{km}\le2z_m,\qquad z\in\mathcal Z_d,
\end{aligned}
\]
with rows over $k$, $i\in[n_k]$ and $y\in\mathcal X$, has the value
$\min_{|S|\le d}V(S)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{sketch}
The diagonal rows give $a_i^{(k)}\ge0$, and $g=0$, $a=\mathbf1$,
$\lambda=0$ has value $2$, so $\rho_k\lambda_k\le2$ at an optimum. At
$z=\mathbf1_S$ the choice $w_{km}=\lambda_kz_m$ is then feasible and
optimal, which recovers the detector program of the theory note.
\end{sketch}

For a piecewise-linear loss this is a mixed-integer linear program with
$N^2$ rows and two prices; for the hinge loss,
\Nref{Proposition}{prop:matching} reduces it to \Eqref{eq:milp}, with one
price and $n_0n_1$ rows.

\para{Open problems.}
(i)~Matching bounds and lazy generation of pair rows, starting from
cross-class nearest neighbours, for large $n$.
(ii)~Certificate size: how many matchings are needed to prove
optimality? Under the margin of \Nref{Proposition}{prop:recovery}, does a
polynomial number suffice? This would be the computational counterpart
of recovery.
(iii)~The first experiment replaces outer approximation by
\Eqref{eq:milp} on the 100-dimensional study.

\section{Direction C: randomized selection}\label{sec:random}
\para{Reference theory.} Linear programming duality for finite games,
randomized defenses in adversarial
classification~\cite{pinot2020randomization,meunier2021mixed},
feature-deletion games~\cite{globerson2006nightmare}, and the problem of
extending consistent marginals to a joint
distribution~\cite{vorobev1962consistent}.

\para{Route.} The $\ell_1$ cost splits across coordinates, so for every
projection an attack is summarized by its coordinate-wise cost vector.
A defender who mixes tangents of $V_S$ faces an attacker whose best
reply spends the whole budget on one coordinate; linear programming
duality turns the defender's problem into the attacker's budget
allocation. For the value to be tight, the attacker must realize every
projected least favorable pair at once, a marginal problem that is
solvable when the candidate subsets are disjoint.

\para{The game.} Throughout this section $\rho=\rho_0+\rho_1$ and
$V_S(r)=2m_S(r)$ is the hinge value of $S$ at total radius $r$. The
defender draws a subset $S$ with $|S|\le d$ and a detector
$f\in\mathcal F_S$ from a distribution $p$; the adversary chooses
$(Q_0,Q_1)\in\mathcal P_0\times\mathcal P_1$ knowing $p$ but not the
draw. Let $\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{rand}}$ be the defender's best
worst-case expected risk. It is at most $\mathrm{OPT}=\min_{|S|\le d}V(S)$
and at least $\sup_Q\min_{|S|\le d}[2-D_\ell(Q_0[S],Q_1[S])]$.

\begin{lemma}[Costs split; proved]\label{lem:split}
For $(Q_0,Q_1)\in\mathcal P_0\times\mathcal P_1$ reached by plans
$\gamma_k$ within the budgets, let $r_m=\sum_k\int|x[m]-y[m]|\,d\gamma_k$. Then
$\sum_mr_m\le\rho$, and for every $S$ the projected attack costs
$\sum_k\mathcal W_1(\widehat{\mathbb P}_k[S],Q_k[S])\le r(S):=\sum_{m\in S}r_m$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The $\ell_1$ cost is the sum of the coordinate costs, and projecting
$\gamma_k$ gives a plan between the projections.
\end{proof}

By the proof of \Nref{Proposition}{prop:witness}, each price $\lambda$
with witness $h$ gives a detector of $\mathcal F_S$ whose risk under an
attack of projected cost $r$ is at most $A+\Lambda r$, with
$A=2(1-\beta_\lambda)$ and $\Lambda=2\lambda$; and $V_S(r)$ is the
minimum of these tangents over $\lambda$.

\begin{theorem}[Achievable randomized value; proved]\label{thm:random}
Let $T$ be a finite family of tangents $(S_t,A_t,\Lambda_t)$. The
best mixture over $T$ guarantees worst-case expected risk at most
\[
\min_{p}\ \max_{m\in[D]}\Bigl\{\sum_tp_tA_t+\rho\sum_{t:\,m\in S_t}p_t\Lambda_t\Bigr\}
=\max_{r\ge0,\ \mathbf1^\top r\le\rho}\ \min_{t\in T}\bigl\{A_t+\Lambda_tr(S_t)\bigr\}
=:W_T.
\]
As $T$ grows to all tangents, $W_T$ decreases to
$\overline W=\max_r\min_{|S|\le d}V_S(r(S))$, and
$\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{rand}}\le\overline W\le\mathrm{OPT}$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
By \Nref{Lemma}{lem:split}, the expected risk of a mixture $p$ under any
attack is at most $\sum_tp_t(A_t+\Lambda_tr(S_t))$ for the attack's cost
vector $r$. This is linear in $r$ with nonnegative slopes, so its
maximum over $\{r\ge0,\mathbf1^\top r\le\rho\}$ is attained at some
$r=\rho e_m$, which gives the left side. The left side is the value of
a finite bilinear game between the simplex and a polytope, so linear
programming duality gives the equality. Adding tangents lowers $W_T$, and the minimum over all tangents of
$S$ is $V_S(r(S))$; the convergence of the maxima follows from Dini's
theorem on the compact set of budgets. Finally, $\overline W\le\min_S\max_rV_S(r(S))=\mathrm{OPT}$
because $V_S$ is nondecreasing and $r(S)\le\rho$.
\end{proof}

The sum $\sum_{t:\,m\in S_t}p_t\Lambda_t$ is the price exposure of
coordinate $m$. The attacker spends its whole budget on the most exposed
coordinate, and the defender's mixture spreads exposure across
coordinates; a fixed selection exposes its $d$ coordinates fully. This
is ``mixing helps the defender'' in its strategic form, and it needs no
minimax theorem beyond finite duality.

\begin{theorem}[Disjoint candidates; proved]\label{thm:waterfill}
If the candidate subsets are pairwise disjoint, in particular if $d=1$,
then $\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{rand}}=\overline W$ with the minimum in
$\overline W$ taken over the candidates, and $\overline W$ is given by
water-filling:
$\overline W=\max\{t:\sum_SV_S^{-1}(t)\le\rho\}$, where
$V_S^{-1}(t)=\inf\{r\ge0:V_S(r)\ge t\}$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The upper bound is \Nref{Theorem}{thm:random}. Since each $V_S$ is
continuous and nondecreasing, an optimal budget $r^\star$ equalizes the
candidates, which gives the water-filling formula. For the lower bound,
take for each candidate $S$ a least favorable pair at total budget
$r^\star(S)$, split between the classes in the ratio $\rho_0:\rho_1$
(\Nref{Proposition}{prop:matching} allows any split). Lemma~2.1 of the
theory note describes it by a plan from each source point. Move each
source point independently in each candidate block according to these
plans. The cost adds over blocks, so both classes stay within budget,
and the projection on each $S$ is its least favorable pair. Every
detector reading $S$ then has risk at least
$V_S(r^\star(S))\ge\overline W$, and so does every mixture.
\end{proof}

For example, take one point per class, $x^{(0)}=(0,0)$ and
$x^{(1)}=(1,1)$, $\rho=1/2$ and $d=1$. Each coordinate has
$V_{\{m\}}(r)=2\min\{r,1\}$, so the fixed optimum is $1$, while
water-filling gives $r^\star=(1/4,1/4)$ and $\overline W=1/2$. With $K$
disjoint candidates of the same separated type, the gain is a factor
$K$. \Tabref{tab:random} shows the effect on the instances of
\Tabref{tab:gap}: the randomized value is well below both the fixed
optimum and the relaxation.

The gain has a precise limit: it exists only against an adversary who
commits to one attack with one shared budget.

\begin{proposition}[No gain against sampling error; proved]\label{prop:nature}
In the setting of \Nref{Theorem}{thm:honest}, let a mixture $p$ use
detectors with the certificates of \Nref{Proposition}{prop:honest}. On the
coverage event, the certificate of the mixture is
$\sum_tp_t\bigl(A_t+\Lambda_t\sum_k\varepsilon_k\bigr)$, an average of
fixed certificates, and hence no smaller than the best of them.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Sampling error does not split a budget across coordinates: the coverage
event allows every projection to be off by its full radius at once, so
each term is bounded separately.
\end{proof}

Randomized selection is therefore a game-theoretic object. Its natural
applications have a strategic adversary who must commit before the
defender's coin, such as inspection, screening or sensor scheduling, and
its guarantee holds on average over that coin.

\para{Open problems.}
For overlapping candidates the attacker needs least favorable pairs
that agree on the shared coordinates and whose coordinate costs respect
one split of the budget. For decomposable families, pairs that agree
can be glued into one attack~\cite{vorobev1962consistent}; for other
families they need not be, and agreement itself may fail.

\begin{conjecture}[Tightness]\label{conj:tight}
$\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{rand}}<\overline W$ on some instances when the
candidates are all subsets of size $d\ge2$; equality holds when the
least favorable pairs at the optimal budget can be chosen to agree on
intersections of a decomposable family.
\end{conjecture}

\noindent Computing $\overline W$ calls for column generation on the
linear program of \Nref{Theorem}{thm:random}. Given the current budget
vector $r$, the pricing problem is $\min_{|S|\le d}V_S(r(S))$, which is
\Eqref{eq:milp} with $\rho\lambda$ replaced by $r^\top w$ and a bound on
$\lambda$. General losses give two prices per tangent and otherwise the
same construction.

\section{Priorities and plan}\label{sec:plan}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Honest radii} (\Secref{sec:honest}) offer the strongest
story at the lowest cost: selection is what makes a Wasserstein robust
test valid in high dimension. \Nref{Theorem}{thm:honest} and
\Nref{Proposition}{prop:collapse} are proved up to the standard coverage
lemma. Next come explicit constants for $d\le2$, \Tabref{tab:noise}
with honest radii, and recovery experiments.
\item \emph{Matching interdiction} (\Secref{sec:interdiction}) starts
with \Eqref{eq:milp} in the core package for the hinge loss, compared
with outer approximation on the 100-dimensional study, then adds the
matching bounds. The honest tolerance is free but saves little.
\item \emph{Randomized selection} (\Secref{sec:random}) is exploratory.
Its two theorems are proved; the marginal problem behind
\Nref{Conjecture}{conj:tight} and a column-generation prototype come
next, together with an application that has a strategic adversary.
\end{enumerate}
