\begin{abstract}
We study budgeted feature selection for binary hypothesis testing under
class-conditional Wasserstein uncertainty. For finite, nonnegative,
nondecreasing convex losses, an exact finite formulation constructs a
whole-space test from every feasible solution. Scaling coordinate transport
prices by the risk gives dual certificate fractions that are exact on binary
selections. Every chosen subset receives a full continuous solve, producing
an actual feasible-test upper bound and a globally valid tangent. Retaining
the safe fraction from the same dual result strengthens the lower model
without another inner solve. Shared binary products give an exact linear
master formulation. A cheap
Gaussian-process predictor uses completed solves to rank a small binary
pool; a global integer master supplies the certified lower bound.
Predictions determine which subset to try, but never replace an inner solve
or provide certificates. Verified solve accuracies imply finite termination
at the requested risk precision, with an evaluation bound determined by the
data movement weights. Population coverage requires separate calibration.
\end{abstract}

\section{The selection problem}\label{sec:problem}
We can observe at most $d$ of $D$ features. The choice of features and the test
using them must be optimized together: a good subset is one on which a test can
keep both classwise losses small when the distributions move within their
specified uncertainty sets.

Pool the observations from the two classes into $x_1,\ldots,x_N\in\mathbb R^D$.
Observation $i$ has class $c_i\in\{0,1\}$ and weight $q_i>0$.
We encode its class by the sign $y_i=2c_i-1$: $y_i=-1$ for class zero
and $y_i=+1$ for class one. Both $c_i$ and $y_i$ are fixed, observed labels,
not optimization variables.
Write $I_k=\{i:c_i=k\}$. The empirical distributions and uncertainty sets are
\[
\widehat P_k=\sum_{i\in I_k}q_i\delta_{x_i},\qquad
\sum_{i\in I_k}q_i=1,\qquad
\mathcal U_k=\{P:W_1(P,\widehat P_k)\le\theta_k\},\quad k=0,1.
\]
Here $\delta_x$ denotes unit mass at $x$. The distance $W_1$ is the minimum
expected $L^1$ distance needed to transport one distribution to the other;
the distributions have finite first moments. The radii $\theta_k>0$ and feature
scales are fixed before selection. Each class has total weight one, so the
pooled weights sum to two.

For an integer budget $0\le d\le D$, a binary vector $z$ selects $S(z)=\{j:z_j=1\}$, with
$\mathcal Z_d=\{z\in\{0,1\}^D:\mathbf1^\top z\le d\}$.
A measurable score $g$ depends only on the selected coordinates. A positive score favors
class zero; the losses are therefore $\Phi(-g)$ for class zero and $\Phi(g)$
for class one. The problem is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:target}
\begin{aligned}
V(z)&=\inf_{g=g(x_{S(z)})}
\left\{\sup_{P\in\mathcal U_0}\mathbb E_P\Phi(-g)
       +\sup_{P\in\mathcal U_1}\mathbb E_P\Phi(g)\right\},\\
\mathrm{OPT}&=\min_{z\in\mathcal Z_d}V(z).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Thus $V(z)$ already includes the best possible test for the chosen features;
selection does not hold a previously fitted test fixed.

The function $\Phi:\mathbb R\to[0,\infty)$ is finite, convex and nondecreasing.
Nondecreasing means that a larger score against the true class cannot reduce
its loss. We consider nonconstant losses with $\Phi(0)>0$. If $\Phi(0)=0$ or
the loss is constant, the constant test already solves the problem.
Specific formulas for $\Phi$ are not needed for the construction below;
examples are given in Section~\ref{sec:examples}.
The constant score $g=0$ provides the common benchmark
\[
0\le V(z)\le B:=2\Phi(0).
\]

\section{A finite problem that still designs the whole test}\label{sec:finite}
The same pooled observations serve as candidate target locations. To compare
source $i$ with target $\ell$, use one vector of coordinatewise distances:
\[
\mathbf d_{i\ell}=|x_i-x_\ell|\in\mathbb R_+^D,\qquad
d_z(x_i,x_\ell)=\mathbf d_{i\ell}^{\top}z.
\]
Absolute value is taken coordinatewise. The vector stores the geometry once;
its inner product with $z$ is the distance using the selected features.

Let $u_\ell$ be the score assigned to target $\ell$. Let $a_i$ be a loss
allowance at source $i$, and let $\lambda_k\ge0$ be the price per unit of
transport for class $k$. Write $q=(q_i)$, $\theta=(\theta_0,\theta_1)^\top$,
$\lambda=(\lambda_0,\lambda_1)^\top$, and
$J(a,\lambda)=q^\top a+\theta^\top\lambda$.

\begin{samepage}
\begin{theorem}[Exact finite formulation]\label{thm:finite}
For every binary $z$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:finite}
\begin{aligned}
V(z)=\min_{a,u,\lambda}\quad &J(a,\lambda)\\
\text{subject to}\quad
&\Phi(y_i u_\ell)\le a_i+
  \lambda_{c_i}\mathbf d_{i\ell}^{\top}z,
  \qquad i,\ell=1,\ldots,N,\\
&\lambda\ge0,\qquad a,u\in\mathbb R^N.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The minimum is attained. Every feasible solution yields a measurable test
on the entire observation space, using only the selected coordinates, with
robust risk at most $J(a,\lambda)$.
\end{theorem}
\end{samepage}
The inequality in \eqref{eq:finite} has a direct meaning: after moving from
source $i$ to target $\ell$, the loss is at most its source allowance plus the
transport price. The source retains its class $c_i$ and sign $y_i$, while
$u_\ell$ is the score at the target. Explicitly,
\[
\Phi(y_i u_\ell)=
\begin{cases}
\Phi(-u_\ell),&c_i=0,\\
\Phi(u_\ell),&c_i=1.
\end{cases}
\]
Thus the loss uses the source class even when the target observation originally
belongs to the other class; either class can send mass to any target location.

The whole-space conclusion is essential. It allows a finite feasible objective
to be used as an upper bound for the original test problem. Its proof blends
two source scores, one from each class. Convexity controls the blended losses,
and the triangle inequality controls the required transport allowances.
The construction and both directions of the equality are given in
Appendix~\ref{app:finite}; no particular inverse or closed form of $\Phi$ is used.

\section{Use the risk to control which coordinates are available}\label{sec:prices}
Taking $\ell=i$ in \eqref{eq:finite} gives $a_i\ge\Phi(y_i u_i)\ge0$.
Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:prices}
\theta^\top\lambda\le J(a,\lambda).
\end{equation}
The transport-price contribution is part of the total risk, so it cannot
exceed that risk. This inequality supplies the bound needed to connect
feature selection with continuous test design.

Introduce two vectors $w_0,w_1\in\mathbb R_+^D$ of coordinate prices.
On a binary selection we can take $w_{kj}=\lambda_k z_j$: an unobserved
coordinate has price zero. To obtain a useful continuous extension, use
the risk bound $\eta$ to constrain these prices:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:risk}
\begin{aligned}
v(z)=\min_{\eta,a,u,\lambda,w_0,w_1}\quad &\eta\\
\text{subject to}\quad
&\Phi(y_i u_\ell)\le a_i+\mathbf d_{i\ell}^{\top}w_{c_i},
  \qquad \forall i,\ell,\\
&J(a,\lambda)\le\eta,\qquad \lambda\ge0,\\
&0\le w_k\le\lambda_k\mathbf1,\qquad k=0,1,\\
&\theta_0w_0+\theta_1w_1\le\eta z.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Vector inequalities hold coordinatewise; $a,u,\eta$ are real variables.
The first line designs the test using the coordinate prices. The next lines
bound its risk and keep each coordinate price below the class price. The
last line makes an unselected coordinate unavailable and ties the allowed
prices to the risk being minimized.

For binary $z$, an optimizer of \eqref{eq:finite} enters \eqref{eq:risk}
with $\eta=J$ and $w_k=\lambda_k z$, by \eqref{eq:prices}.
Conversely, a feasible point of \eqref{eq:risk} has zero prices on unselected
coordinates and $w_k\le\lambda_k z$. The same $a,u,\lambda$ therefore satisfy
\eqref{eq:finite}, with objective at most $\eta$. Hence $v(z)=V(z)$ at every
binary selection. For fractional $z\in[0,1]^D$, \eqref{eq:risk} defines the
extension used to construct lower bounds.

Fixing $z$ makes \eqref{eq:risk} a convex problem. This alone does not prove
that its value $v(z)$ is convex in $z$, since the last line contains the
product $\eta z$. The next step establishes that property and supplies
information that remains useful when the feature subset changes.

\section{One solved subset gives lower bounds for all subsets}\label{sec:dual}
Consider a nonnegative matrix $\Pi$ with $\Pi_{i\ell}$ units of mass sent from
source $i$ to target $\ell$. Its rows retain the source weights,
$\Pi\mathbf1=q$. The masses received from the two classes and their
coordinatewise movements are
\[
p_{k\ell}=\sum_{i\in I_k}\Pi_{i\ell},\qquad
A_k(\Pi)=\sum_{i\in I_k,\ell}\Pi_{i\ell}\mathbf d_{i\ell}
\in\mathbb R_+^D.
\]
At a target receiving both classes, one score must serve both. The least
weighted loss that this common score can achieve is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:psi}
\Psi(p_0,p_1)=\inf_s\{p_0\Phi(-s)+p_1\Phi(s)\},
\qquad H(\Pi)=\sum_\ell\Psi(p_{0\ell},p_{1\ell}).
\end{equation}
The infimum permits limiting scores when one class has zero mass.
$H$ measures the loss left after the best score is chosen at every target.
It is nonnegative. Scaling all masses by $t\ge0$ scales $H$ by $t$; this
property concerns the masses and does not require $\Phi$ itself to be homogeneous.

To use a matrix that may move more mass than the original budget allows,
introduce a nonnegative correction vector $\mu\in\mathbb R_+^D$.
The pair $(\Pi,\mu)$ must satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:certificate}
\Pi\ge0,\qquad \Pi\mathbf1=q,\qquad \mu\ge0,\qquad
\mathbf1^\top\bigl(A_k(\Pi)-\theta_k\mu\bigr)_+\le\theta_k,
\quad k=0,1.
\end{equation}
Here $t_+=\max(t,0)$, applied coordinatewise to a vector.
After $\theta_k\mu_j$ is deducted from movement in coordinate $j$, the
remaining positive movements must fit inside the class budget $\theta_k$.
These conditions contain no $z$: the same pair can be used at any selection.
The matrix need not be a transport plan inside the original uncertainty
balls. The correction is what makes it useful as a risk lower bound.

The reason for the correction in the denominator can be seen directly.
For $0\le z\le1$, \eqref{eq:certificate} implies
$z^\top A_k(\Pi)\le\theta_k(1+\mu^\top z)$.
For any feasible point of \eqref{eq:finite} on a binary selection,
\[
\begin{aligned}
H(\Pi)
&\le\sum_{i,\ell}\Pi_{i\ell}\Phi(y_i u_\ell)\\
&\le q^\top a+\sum_k\lambda_k z^\top A_k(\Pi)\\
&\le J(a,\lambda)+(\mu^\top z)\theta^\top\lambda\\
&\le(1+\mu^\top z)J(a,\lambda).
\end{aligned}
\]
The four steps use, respectively, best-score loss, finite loss constraints,
the corrected transport budgets, and \eqref{eq:prices}.
Thus $H(\Pi)/(1+\mu^\top z)$ is a lower bound on $V(z)$.

\begin{samepage}
\begin{theorem}[Exact representation and convexity]\label{thm:dual}
For every $z\in[0,1]^D$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ratio}
v(z)=\max_{\Pi,\mu\ \text{satisfying }\eqref{eq:certificate}}
\frac{H(\Pi)}{1+\mu^\top z}.
\end{equation}
The maximum is attained, $v$ is convex, and $v(z)=V(z)$ on binary selections.
\end{theorem}
\end{samepage}
The preceding inequalities explain why each fraction is a valid lower bound.
Equality with $v$ requires more: minimizing the Lagrangian of
\eqref{eq:risk} gives the row masses, transport inequalities and one
normalization equation. Rescaling those multipliers gives exactly
\eqref{eq:ratio}. Appendix~\ref{app:duality} derives the objective and every
constraint by eliminating one group of variables at a time.
For fixed $(\Pi,\mu)$, the fraction is convex because its numerator is
nonnegative and its denominator is a positive affine function. The maximum
of these convex lower functions is therefore convex.

\section{Choosing which binary subset to evaluate}\label{sec:algorithm}
Fix a requested risk accuracy $\varepsilon>0$. Let $\delta\le\varepsilon/4$
be the allowed inner risk gap, and $\xi\le\varepsilon/4$ the allowed
master candidate gap, both nonnegative. Previous evaluations help
choose the next binary subset. Every chosen subset then receives a full
solve of \eqref{eq:risk}, with all continuous variables reoptimized.

\paragraph{Solve the subset and store a global cut.}
At $z^s\in\mathcal Z_d$, the inner solve returns a finite feasible solution
$(a^s,u^s,\lambda^s)$ and its whole-space test. Its verified risk bound is
$R_s=J(a^s,\lambda^s)$. Retaining the constant test ensures $R_s\le B$.
The dual solve returns a pair $(\Pi^s,\mu^s)$ satisfying \eqref{eq:certificate}.
Write its verified fraction as $f_s(z)=h_s/[1+(\mu^s)^\top z]$, with
$0\le h_s\le H(\Pi^s)$. The certified value $\ell_s\le f_s(z^s)$ must satisfy
\[
\ell_s\le V(z^s)\le R_s,\qquad
0\le R_s-\ell_s\le\delta\le\varepsilon/4.
\]
In exact arithmetic $\ell_s=f_s(z^s)$; any numerical downward shift is
included in this gap. The fraction's gradient gives the cut
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cut}
g_s=-\frac{h_s\mu^s}{[1+(\mu^s)^\top z^s]^2},\qquad
L_s(z)=\ell_s+g_s^\top(z-z^s)\le v(z).
\end{equation}
Convexity puts the tangent below $f_s$, while \eqref{eq:ratio} puts $f_s$
below $v$. Store this cut and the actual feasible test together.
Keep the fraction's shape as well, scaling its numerator to the certified
anchor value:
\[
F_s(z)=[\ell_s]_+\frac{1+(\mu^s)^\top z^s}{1+(\mu^s)^\top z}
\le f_s(z)\le v(z).
\]
This function uses the same completed dual result. Its numerator remains
between zero and $h_s$, as shown in Appendix~\ref{app:algorithm}.
In exact arithmetic $F_s=f_s$. Away from its anchor, a tangent can become
negative while its fraction stays positive. Retaining $F_s$ preserves this
information at the cost of evaluating a fraction; retain $L_s$ as well
when numerical downward shifts are present.

\paragraph{Rank a small set of binary proposals.}
After $t$ full evaluations, the actual test bounds and retained lower functions give
\[
U_t=\min\{B,R_1,\ldots,R_t\},\qquad
a_t(z)=\max\{0,L_s(z),F_s(z):s=1,\ldots,t\}.
\]
Here $U_t$ is the global upper bound and $a_t(z)$ is a lower estimate of
each subset's optimal risk. For $t=0$, set $U_0=B$ and $a_0=0$.
For the same completed evaluations, retaining the fractions only raises the
lower model, strengthening the improvement screen and the global bound.
Adding coordinates cannot increase $V$, since a test can ignore them.
The retained lower functions are also coordinatewise nonincreasing.
Thus both minima can be attained on full-budget subsets
$\mathbf1^\top z=d$, which the implementation uses.

Fit a cheap Gaussian-process predictor to the observed pairs
$(z^s,R_s)$. A fixed similarity between subsets lets an evaluated subset
inform nearby subsets, which receive lower predictive uncertainty when
more strongly constrained by these observations. Denote the predicted
risk by $m_t(z)$ and its predictive standard deviation by $\sigma_t(z)$.
Both are used for ranking, not as guaranteed risk bounds; their construction
is given in Appendix~\ref{app:prediction}.

Form a small finite pool $\mathcal C_t\subseteq\mathcal Z_d$ of full-budget subsets using a few
coordinate swaps and random proposals. Keep subsets whose lower estimate
allows sufficient improvement, and choose
\[
z^{t+1}\in\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\substack{z\in\mathcal C_t\\
a_t(z)\le U_t-\varepsilon/2}}
\bigl[m_t(z)-\sigma_t(z)\bigr].
\]
The score favors low predicted risk while allowing uncertain subsets to
be tried. The screen exceeds the inner error $\delta\le\varepsilon/4$,
so a resolved subset cannot be chosen again. The chosen subset is then
fully solved: only its actual $R_s$ can update $U_t$, and only its verified
dual result supplies a new cut. The predictor does neither.

\paragraph{Use the global master to certify stopping.}
The master minimizes the lower model over all allowed subsets:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:master}
\begin{aligned}
\min_{z\in\mathcal Z_d,\eta}\quad&\eta\\
\text{subject to}\quad&0\le\eta\le U_t,\qquad\mathbf1^\top z=d,\\
&\eta\ge L_s(z),\qquad \eta\ge F_s(z)\quad(s=1,\ldots,t).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The current best test makes this master feasible, and the bound
$\eta\le U_t$ preserves the minimum of $a_t$.
For binary $z$, use shared products $b_j=\eta z_j$. The bounds
\[
0\le b_j\le U_tz_j,\qquad b_j\le\eta,\qquad
b_j\ge\eta-U_t(1-z_j)\quad(j=1,\ldots,D).
\]
enforce these products exactly. Each constraint $\eta\ge F_s(z)$ is then
one linear row,
\[
\eta+(\mu^s)^\top b\ge[\ell_s]_+\bigl[1+(\mu^s)^\top z^s\bigr].
\]
Thus the stronger master is a mixed-integer linear problem with only $D$
additional variables shared by all stored fractions.
Appendix~\ref{app:algorithm} proves both equivalences.
The master returns a binary $z_{\mathrm M}$ and a certified global lower bound.
Update $\mathrm{LB}_t$ with the maximum of its previous value and this bound;
all earlier cuts and fractions remain. Stop if $U_t-\mathrm{LB}_t\le\varepsilon$.
Otherwise require $a_t(z_{\mathrm M})-\mathrm{LB}_t\le\xi\le\varepsilon/4$
after the update. Then
\[
a_t(z_{\mathrm M})\le\mathrm{LB}_t+\xi
<U_t-\varepsilon+\xi\le U_t-3\varepsilon/4,
\]
so the master supplies an eligible candidate. Refresh it every three full
evaluations and whenever the pool has no eligible subset. If a solve cannot
attain its required accuracy within the budget, return the available bounds
and test with that status.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Full inner solves with retained dual fractions}\label{alg:selection}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $\varepsilon>0$; $0\le\delta\le\varepsilon/4$; $0\le\xi\le\varepsilon/4$.
\State Set $t=0$, $U_0=B$, $\mathrm{LB}_0=0$; retain the constant test and initialize empty histories and the predictor.
\While{$U_t-\mathrm{LB}_t>\varepsilon$}
\State Form the small binary pool $\mathcal C_t$ and keep proposals with $a_t(z)\le U_t-\varepsilon/2$.
\If{no proposal is eligible, or three full evaluations have occurred since the last master solve}
\State Solve \eqref{eq:master} and update $\mathrm{LB}_t$ with its certified bound.
\State If $U_t-\mathrm{LB}_t\le\varepsilon$, return the retained best test; otherwise require $a_t(z_{\mathrm M})-\mathrm{LB}_t\le\xi$ and add $z_{\mathrm M}$ to the eligible pool.
\EndIf
\State Choose an eligible proposal minimizing $m_t(z)-\sigma_t(z)$.
\State Set $s=t+1$, $z^s=z$; fully solve \eqref{eq:risk} there, obtaining a feasible test and a verified fraction with $R_s-\ell_s\le\delta$.
\State Compute \eqref{eq:cut} at this evaluated point; store $L_s$, $F_s$, the subset and actual test bound; retain every previous lower function.
\State Update the predictor with the completed observation $(z^s,R_s)$.
\State Set $U_{t+1}=\min\{U_t,R_s\}$, $\mathrm{LB}_{t+1}=\mathrm{LB}_t$, and $t\gets t+1$.
\EndWhile
\State \Return The retained best test and subset, with interval $[\mathrm{LB}_t,U_t]$.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The evaluation bound uses the coordinate movement in the data. Let
$d_{i\ell,j}$ be entry $j$ of $\mathbf d_{i\ell}$ and define
\[
M_j=\max_{k=0,1}
\frac{\sum_{i\in I_k}q_i\max_\ell d_{i\ell,j}}{\theta_k},
\qquad \rho(z,z')=\sum_j M_j|z_j-z'_j|.
\]
Thus $\rho$ measures how selections differ in transport units. Clipping a
feasible correction to $0\le\mu^s\le M$ preserves feasibility; use it before
computing the value and gradient in \eqref{eq:cut}. The positive separation
scale and number of retained coordinate weights are
\[
r_\varepsilon=\frac{\varepsilon/2-\delta}{B}>0,\qquad
M_{(1)}\ge\cdots\ge M_{(D)},\qquad
s_\varepsilon=\min\left\{s\in\{0,\ldots,D\}:
\sum_{j>s}M_{(j)}<r_\varepsilon\right\}.
\]
The proof shows that evaluated subsets are at least $r_\varepsilon$ apart
in $\rho$; $s_\varepsilon$ makes the omitted weight smaller than this distance.
Let $T_\varepsilon$ count completed full binary evaluations begun before
stopping, including the first. Refinements at one selected point count as
one evaluation.

\begin{theorem}[Global optimality and evaluation bound]\label{thm:global}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:selection}, with the stated feasibility, correction and
accuracy requirements and all generated cuts and fractions retained, maintains
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bounds}
\mathrm{LB}_t\le\mathrm{OPT}\le U_t.
\end{equation}
If the required solves complete without a premature budget exit, it returns
a feasible test with robust risk at most $\mathrm{OPT}+\varepsilon$. The number
of completed evaluations satisfies
\[
T_\varepsilon\le
\min\left\{|\mathcal Z_d|,
\sum_{i=0}^{\min(d,s_\varepsilon)}\binom{s_\varepsilon}{i}\right\}
\le2^{s_\varepsilon}.
\]
\end{theorem}
Appendix~\ref{app:algorithm} proves these statements. The predictor affects
which eligible subset is tried, not the validity of the bounds or the count.

\section{Loss choices and the scope of the result}\label{sec:examples}
The loss enters through $\Phi$ in the continuous problem and through
$\Psi$ in the dual objective. Examples covered by the same derivation are
shown below; boundary values use continuous limits.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Loss & $\Phi(s)$ & $\Psi(p_0,p_1)$\\
\midrule
Half hinge & $\tfrac12(1+s)_+$ & $\min(p_0,p_1)$\\
Logistic & $\log(1+e^s)$ &
$(p_0+p_1)\log(p_0+p_1)-p_0\log p_0-p_1\log p_1$\\
Exponential & $e^s$ & $2\sqrt{p_0p_1}$\\
Squared hinge & $(1+s)_+^2$ & $4p_0p_1/(p_0+p_1)$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
For example, half-hinge masses $p_0=0.2$ and $p_1=0.5$ leave loss $0.2$:
the best common score favors the larger mass but cannot distinguish the
smaller mass at the same location. Appendix~\ref{app:losses} derives all four
formulas. For half hinge, a score clipped to $[-1,1]$ gives randomized
class-zero probability $(1+g)/2$, so $V/2$ is the equal-prior worst-case error.
Other losses define surrogate objectives. The current implementation uses
half hinge and logistic; the other rows illustrate the theoretical scope.

The loss formulas need not be fixed before deriving the algorithm.
The transport geometry still matters: the whole-space construction uses
the triangle inequality, and the coordinate-price formulation uses the
$L^1$ decomposition.
The certified interval concerns the stated robust optimization problem;
whether its uncertainty sets cover an unknown population is a separate
statistical question.

\section{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:finite}: the finite formulation}\label{app:finite}
Under the assumptions of Section~\ref{sec:problem}, let $T(z)$ be the infimum of the finite program in \eqref{eq:finite}. Equality with $V(z)$ requires two directions. Restricting adversarial transport to the observed target pool gives $T(z)\le V(z)$. For the reverse direction, we extend any finite feasible solution to a whole-space test with the same loss allowances, giving $V(z)\le T(z)$. The second direction explains why solving the finite problem is sufficient to construct a test. We prove these inequalities first, then establish attainment.

\subsection{Restrict the adversary, then identify the finite objective}
Fix binary $z$ and $S=\{j:z_j=1\}$. Write $P_S$ for the distribution of the selected coordinates under $P$. A transport in the selected coordinates can be realized in the original space by leaving each source's unselected coordinates fixed. The following identities verify its cost and test loss:
\begin{equation}\label{app:projection}
\begin{aligned}
d_z(x,x')&:=\sum_{j=1}^Dz_j|x_j-x'_j|=\|x_S-x'_S\|_1\le\|x-x'\|_1,\\
W_1(P_S,\widehat P_{k,S})&\le W_1(P,\widehat P_k),\\
\|x_i-(x_{\ell,S},x_{i,S^c})\|_1&=d_z(x_i,x_\ell),\\
g(x_{\ell,S},x_{i,S^c})&=g(x_\ell).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Lifting retains the unselected source coordinates, so it preserves both selected transport cost and test loss. If source projections coincide, split their transported mass according to their weights and retain their source identities. The same construction lifts arbitrary projected targets and preserves finite first moments.

For scores $u=(u_\ell)_{\ell=1}^N$, define $Q_k(u)$ as the largest class-$k$ loss obtained by sending mass to the projected pool. The variable $\Pi_{i\ell}$ sends mass from source $i\in I_k$ to target $\ell$; only class-$k$ rows occur in this calculation:
\begin{equation}\label{app:finite-lp}
\begin{aligned}
Q_k(u)=\max_{\Pi\ge0}\quad&\sum_{i\in I_k,\ell}\Pi_{i\ell}\Phi(y_i u_\ell)\\
\text{subject to}\quad&\sum_\ell\Pi_{i\ell}=q_i\quad(i\in I_k),\\
&\sum_{i\in I_k,\ell}\Pi_{i\ell}d_z(x_i,x_\ell)\le\theta_k.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
For any original test, define $u(g)=(g(x_1),\ldots,g(x_N))$. Lift each feasible transport using \eqref{app:projection}: its full-space distribution lies in $\mathcal U_k$ and its loss is $\sum_{i\in I_k,\ell}\Pi_{i\ell}\Phi(y_i g(x_\ell))$. Thus
\begin{equation}\label{app:induced-score-bound}
Q_k(u(g))\le\sup_{P\in\mathcal U_k}\mathbb E_P\Phi((2k-1)g).
\end{equation}

Staying at the source is feasible, and $0\le\Pi_{i\ell}\le q_i$ makes the feasible set compact. To express this restricted worst-case loss using the finite primal variables, derive its LP dual. Assign unrestricted row multipliers $a_i$ and a nonnegative budget multiplier $\lambda_k$. Because this is a maximization problem, the Lagrangian is
\begin{equation}\label{app:finite-lp-lagrangian}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L_k={}&\sum_{i\in I_k,\ell}\Pi_{i\ell}\Phi(y_i u_\ell)
+\sum_{i\in I_k}a_i\left(q_i-\sum_\ell\Pi_{i\ell}\right)\\
&+\lambda_k\left(\theta_k-\sum_{i\in I_k,\ell}\Pi_{i\ell}d_z(x_i,x_\ell)\right)\\
={}&\sum_{i\in I_k}q_i a_i+\theta_k\lambda_k
+\sum_{i\in I_k,\ell}\Pi_{i\ell}
[\Phi(y_i u_\ell)-a_i-\lambda_kd_z(x_i,x_\ell)].
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
For a feasible transport the row terms vanish and the budget term is nonnegative, so this Lagrangian bounds its loss above. After these constraints enter the Lagrangian, its supremum retains only $\Pi\ge0$. A positive mass coefficient gives $+\infty$; if every coefficient is nonpositive, $\Pi=0$ attains the supremum:
\begin{equation}\label{app:finite-lp-supremum}
\sup_{\Pi\ge0}\mathcal L_k=
\begin{cases}
\sum_{i\in I_k}q_i a_i+\theta_k\lambda_k,
&\Phi(y_i u_\ell)\le a_i+\lambda_kd_z(x_i,x_\ell)\quad(i\in I_k,\ \forall\ell),\\
+\infty,&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
Minimize this upper bound over $a_i\in\mathbb R$ and $\lambda_k\ge0$. Finite linear programming strong duality gives
\begin{equation}\label{app:finite-lp-dual}
Q_k(u)=\min_{\substack{a_i\in\mathbb R,\ \lambda_k\ge0\\
\Phi(y_i u_\ell)\le a_i+\lambda_kd_z(x_i,x_\ell)\ (i\in I_k,\ \forall\ell)}}
\left[\sum_{i\in I_k}q_i a_i+\theta_k\lambda_k\right].
\end{equation}

Both classwise dual objectives use the same score vector $u$. Minimizing their sum over $u$ is exactly the finite program \eqref{eq:finite}, so $T(z)=\inf_u[Q_0(u)+Q_1(u)]$.
Restricting the infimum to induced scores and using \eqref{app:induced-score-bound} gives
\begin{equation}\label{app:finite-lower}
\begin{aligned}
T(z)&=\inf_u[Q_0(u)+Q_1(u)]\\
&\le\inf_g[Q_0(u(g))+Q_1(u(g))]\le V(z).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Separate scores at coincident projected targets only relax the first infimum.

\subsection{Extend the finite loss bounds to the whole space}
Take a feasible $(a,u,\lambda)$ in \eqref{eq:finite}. We need a score $g$ on the whole space whose losses satisfy
\[
\Phi((2k-1)g(x))\le a_i+\lambda_kd_z(x_i,x)
\quad(i\in I_k,\ k=0,1).
\]
Integrating this inequality will turn the transport budget into the objective $J(a,\lambda)$. To satisfy every source inequality at once, it suffices to satisfy the smallest allowance in each class. Define
\begin{equation}\label{app:envelopes}
\begin{aligned}
E_k(x)&=\min_{i\in I_k}\{a_i+\lambda_kd_z(x_i,x)\},\\
i_k(x)&=\min\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{i\in I_k}\{a_i+\lambda_kd_z(x_i,x)\}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Choose the smallest source index in a tie. At fixed $x$, abbreviate $i_k=i_k(x)$; $c$ is the distance between the chosen sources and $r_k$ their distances to $x$:
\begin{equation}\label{app:source-distances}
c=d_z(x_{i_0},x_{i_1}),\qquad r_k=d_z(x_{i_k},x),\qquad c\le r_0+r_1.
\end{equation}
Substitute source--target pairs $(i_0,i_0)$, $(i_0,i_1)$, $(i_1,i_0)$, and $(i_1,i_1)$ in \eqref{eq:finite}. Their source signs are $-1$ and $+1$, and the own-source distances are zero:
\begin{equation}\label{app:endpoints}
\begin{aligned}
\Phi(-u_{i_0})&\le a_{i_0},&\Phi(-u_{i_1})&\le a_{i_0}+\lambda_0c,\\
\Phi(u_{i_0})&\le a_{i_1}+\lambda_1c,&\Phi(u_{i_1})&\le a_{i_1}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Consider a convex combination with weight $\alpha\in[0,1]$. Convexity bounds the extra class-zero loss by $\lambda_0\alpha c$ and the extra class-one loss by $\lambda_1(1-\alpha)c$. We therefore need $\alpha c\le r_0$ and $(1-\alpha)c\le r_1$. When $c>0$, choose

\begin{equation}\label{app:interpolation}
\alpha=\frac{\min(c,r_0)}c,\qquad g(x)=(1-\alpha)u_{i_0}+\alpha u_{i_1}.
\end{equation}
The triangle inequality ensures that both portions of this distance fit within $r_0,r_1$:
\begin{equation}\label{app:interpolation-allowances}
\begin{aligned}
0\le\alpha\le1,\qquad \alpha c&=\min(c,r_0)\le r_0,\\
(1-\alpha)c&=c-\min(c,r_0)=\max(c-r_0,0)\le r_1.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
In particular, the last inequality uses $c-r_0\le r_1$ and $r_1\ge0$.


Convexity, these four constraints, and \eqref{app:interpolation-allowances} give the two pointwise loss bounds:
\begin{equation}\label{app:loss-control}
\begin{aligned}
\Phi(-g(x))
&\le(1-\alpha)\Phi(-u_{i_0})+\alpha\Phi(-u_{i_1})\\
&\le(1-\alpha)a_{i_0}+\alpha(a_{i_0}+\lambda_0c)
=a_{i_0}+\lambda_0\alpha c\\
&\le a_{i_0}+\lambda_0r_0=E_0(x),\\
\Phi(g(x))
&\le(1-\alpha)\Phi(u_{i_0})+\alpha\Phi(u_{i_1})\\
&\le(1-\alpha)(a_{i_1}+\lambda_1c)+\alpha a_{i_1}
=a_{i_1}+\lambda_1(1-\alpha)c\\
&\le a_{i_1}+\lambda_1r_1=E_1(x).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
If $c=0$, set $g(x)=u_{i_0}$. The first and third endpoint inequalities give instead
\begin{equation}\label{app:zero-distance}
\Phi(-g(x))\le a_{i_0}\le E_0(x),\qquad
\Phi(g(x))\le a_{i_1}+\lambda_1c=a_{i_1}\le E_1(x).
\end{equation}
Finite comparisons of continuous functions make $i_k(x)$ Borel measurable, and hence the piecewise $g$ is Borel measurable. It uses only $x_S$ and lies between $\min_\ell u_\ell$ and $\max_\ell u_\ell$. It extends the loss bounds; interpolation of every prescribed $u_\ell$ is unnecessary.

The construction gives the required sourcewise loss bounds. Their expected source allowances are weighted by $q_i$, and their expected transport distance is at most $\theta_k$. To make these two facts explicit, fix $P\in\mathcal U_k$ and choose a coupling of cost at most $\theta_k$, retaining source indices and splitting coincident atoms according to their weights. Let $\gamma_k$ be its measure on $I_k\times\mathbb R^D$, with target marginal $P$. Its source rows and cost satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{app:coupling-rows}
\gamma_k(\{i\}\times\mathbb R^D)=q_i,\qquad
\int d_z(x_i,x)\,d\gamma_k(i,x)
\le\int\|x_i-x\|_1\,d\gamma_k(i,x)\le\theta_k.
\end{equation}
For each source $i\in I_k$, the constructed score satisfies $\Phi((2k-1)g(x))\le E_k(x)\le a_i+\lambda_kd_z(x_i,x)$. Using both marginals gives
\begin{equation}\label{app:finite-upper}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E_P\Phi((2k-1)g)
&=\int\Phi((2k-1)g(x))\,d\gamma_k(i,x)\\
&\le\int a_i\,d\gamma_k(i,x)+\lambda_k\int d_z(x_i,x)\,d\gamma_k(i,x)\\
&=\sum_{i\in I_k}a_i\gamma_k(\{i\}\times\mathbb R^D)+\lambda_k\int d_z(x_i,x)\,d\gamma_k(i,x)\\
&=\sum_{i\in I_k}q_i a_i+\lambda_k\int d_z(x_i,x)\,d\gamma_k(i,x)\\
&\le\sum_{i\in I_k}q_i a_i+\theta_k\lambda_k.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The bound is independent of $P$, so taking the classwise suprema and adding yields
\[
\sum_{k=0}^1\sup_{P\in\mathcal U_k}\mathbb E_P\Phi((2k-1)g)
\le\sum_{k=0}^1\left[\sum_{i\in I_k}q_i a_i+\theta_k\lambda_k\right]
=J(a,\lambda).
\]
Every finite feasible point therefore yields an original test with risk at most $J$. Taking the infimum gives $V(z)\le T(z)$; \eqref{app:finite-lower} gives the other direction. This proves the value equality and feasible-test claim in Theorem~\ref{thm:finite}.

\subsection{Attainment of the primal minima}\label{app:attainment}
To prove attainment for both \eqref{eq:finite} and \eqref{eq:risk}, we bound all variables on a nonempty objective sublevel set and then use closedness.

The constant point $u=0$, $a_i=\Phi(0)$, $\lambda=0$ is feasible in \eqref{eq:finite} with $J=B$. Adding $w_0=w_1=0$ and $\eta=B$ makes it feasible in \eqref{eq:risk} for every $z\in[0,1]^D$. Thus the objective sublevels $J\le B$ and $J\le\eta\le B$ are nonempty. Own-source constraints give $a_i\ge\Phi(y_i u_i)\ge0$; positive weights and radii then give
\begin{equation}\label{app:bounded}
\begin{aligned}
0\le q_i a_i&\le q^{\top}a\le J\le B
&&\implies0\le a_i\le B/q_i,\\
0\le\theta_k\lambda_k&\le\theta^{\top}\lambda\le J\le B
&&\implies0\le\lambda_k\le B/\theta_k,\\
0\le w_{kj}&\le\lambda_k\le B/\theta_k,
&&0\le J\le\eta\le B\quad\text{in }\eqref{eq:risk}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Choose a fixed source $i_k\in I_k$ for each class. The finite pool distances and \eqref{app:bounded} bound both score signs by the finite constant
\[
\begin{aligned}
C&=\max_{k,\ell}\left\{B/q_{i_k}+(B/\theta_k)\|\mathbf d_{i_k\ell}\|_1\right\},\\
\Phi((2k-1)u_\ell)&\le B/q_{i_k}+(B/\theta_k)\|\mathbf d_{i_k\ell}\|_1\le C.
\end{aligned}
\]
We have bounded both $\Phi(u_\ell)$ and $\Phi(-u_\ell)$. To turn these loss bounds into a bound on the score itself, it remains to show that $\Phi$ grows without bound in the positive direction. Nonconstancy and monotonicity give $s_1<s_2$ with $\Phi(s_2)>\Phi(s_1)$. Set $b=[\Phi(s_2)-\Phi(s_1)]/(s_2-s_1)>0$; convexity gives, for $t>s_2$,
\begin{equation}\label{app:growth}
\frac{\Phi(t)-\Phi(s_2)}{t-s_2}\ge b
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\Phi(t)\ge\Phi(s_2)+b(t-s_2)\longrightarrow\infty.
\end{equation}
Choose $R>0$ with $\Phi(R)>C$, possible by \eqref{app:growth}. Monotonicity gives
\[
u_\ell\ge R\implies\Phi(u_\ell)>C,\qquad
u_\ell\le-R\implies\Phi(-u_\ell)>C;
\qquad |u_\ell|<R.
\]
All variables on these sublevels are bounded. The finite convex loss is continuous, so the sublevels are closed and hence compact. Both continuous objectives attain their minima there. Since a constant test with value $B$ is already feasible, restricting to these sublevels preserves both global minima.

\section{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:dual}: from the risk model to global lower functions}\label{app:duality}
Fix $z\in[0,1]^D$. We want to express the optimal risk $v(z)$ by pairs $(\Pi,\mu)$ that remain feasible when $z$ changes. First minimize the Lagrangian over the test-design variables; the remaining multipliers describe masses, movements and a common scale $\tau$. Dividing out this scale gives the fraction in \eqref{eq:ratio}. Binary equivalence then connects it to $V$, and convexity explains why its tangent is a global lower bound.

\subsection{Eliminate the design variables to obtain the dual}
In \eqref{eq:risk}, the variables $\eta,a,u$ are free and $\lambda,w_0,w_1$ are nonnegative. Attach one nonnegative multiplier to each inequality:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
Constraint & Multiplier\\
\midrule
$\Phi(y_i u_\ell)-a_i-\mathbf d_{i\ell}^{\top}w_{c_i}\le0$ & $\bar\Pi_{i\ell}$\\
$J-\eta\le0$ & $\tau$\\
$w_{kj}-\lambda_k\le0$ & $\bar\beta_{kj}$\\
$\theta_0w_{0j}+\theta_1w_{1j}-\eta z_j\le0$ & $\bar\mu_j$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Here $\bar\Pi\in\mathbb R_+^{N\times N}$, $\tau\ge0$, and $\bar\beta_k,\bar\mu\in\mathbb R_+^D$. Bars indicate multipliers before their masses are normalized. Adding each multiplier times its constraint to the objective gives

\begin{equation}\label{app:lagrangian}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L={}&\eta+\tau(J-\eta)
+\sum_{i,\ell}\bar\Pi_{i\ell}[\Phi(y_i u_\ell)-a_i-\mathbf d_{i\ell}^{\top}w_{c_i}]\\
&+\sum_k\bar\beta_k^{\top}(w_k-\lambda_k\mathbf1)
+\bar\mu^{\top}(\theta_0w_0+\theta_1w_1-\eta z).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

At a primal feasible point, every added term is nonpositive. Thus fixed multipliers give the lower bound

\[
\inf_{\substack{\eta\in\mathbb R,\ a,u\in\mathbb R^N\\\lambda,w_0,w_1\ge0}}\mathcal L
\le\mathcal L(\eta,a,u,\lambda,w_0,w_1)\le\eta.
\]

We maximize this lower bound after computing the infimum. The four constraint groups are already in $\mathcal L$ and are not imposed again; in particular, $a$ and $\eta$ remain free in this minimization.

To expose the coefficient of each design variable, collect the class masses and their movements:

\begin{equation}\label{app:dual-masses}
\bar p_{k\ell}=\sum_{i\in I_k}\bar\Pi_{i\ell},\qquad
A_k(\bar\Pi)=\sum_{i\in I_k,\ell}\bar\Pi_{i\ell}\mathbf d_{i\ell}.
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{app:regrouping}
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{i,\ell}\bar\Pi_{i\ell}a_i
&=\sum_i a_i\sum_\ell\bar\Pi_{i\ell},\\
\sum_{i,\ell}\bar\Pi_{i\ell}\Phi(y_i u_\ell)
&=\sum_\ell[\bar p_{0\ell}\Phi(-u_\ell)+\bar p_{1\ell}\Phi(u_\ell)],\\
\sum_{i,\ell}\bar\Pi_{i\ell}\mathbf d_{i\ell}^{\top}w_{c_i}
&=\sum_k w_k^{\top}A_k(\bar\Pi),\\
\tau J&=\sum_i\tau q_i a_i+\sum_k\tau\theta_k\lambda_k.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Substitution separates $\mathcal L$ into five variable groups:

\begin{equation}\label{app:collected-lagrangian}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L={}&\eta(1-\tau-\bar\mu^{\top}z)
+\sum_i a_i(\tau q_i-\sum_\ell\bar\Pi_{i\ell})\\
&+\sum_\ell[\bar p_{0\ell}\Phi(-u_\ell)+\bar p_{1\ell}\Phi(u_\ell)]\\
&+\sum_k\lambda_k(\tau\theta_k-\mathbf1^{\top}\bar\beta_k)
+\sum_k w_k^{\top}[\bar\beta_k+\theta_k\bar\mu-A_k(\bar\Pi)].
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Now minimize each group. A linear term in a free variable has a finite infimum only when its coefficient is zero; a term in a nonnegative variable requires a nonnegative coefficient:

Here $b\in\mathbb R$ is a scalar coefficient and $t$ a scalar variable.

\[
\inf_{t\in\mathbb R}bt=\begin{cases}0,&b=0,\\-\infty,&b\ne0,\end{cases}
\qquad
\inf_{t\ge0}bt=\begin{cases}0,&b\ge0,\\-\infty,&b<0.\end{cases}
\]

Taking $n\to+\infty$, $t=-nb$ proves the first divergence when $b\ne0$, and $t=n$ proves the second when $b<0$; otherwise the infimum is zero at $t=0$. Applying the first rule to $\eta$ and $a$ gives

\begin{equation}\label{app:free-elimination}
\begin{aligned}
\inf_{\eta\in\mathbb R}\eta(1-\tau-\bar\mu^{\top}z)>-\infty
&\iff\tau+\bar\mu^{\top}z=1,\\
\inf_{a\in\mathbb R^N}\sum_i a_i(\tau q_i-\sum_\ell\bar\Pi_{i\ell})>-\infty
&\iff\bar\Pi\mathbf1=\tau q.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The first equation normalizes the multipliers; the second fixes each source's row mass at $\tau$ times its original weight. The score variables contribute the best possible weighted loss at each target:

\begin{equation}\label{app:score-infimum}
\begin{aligned}
\inf_{u\in\mathbb R^N}\sum_\ell
[\bar p_{0\ell}\Phi(-u_\ell)+\bar p_{1\ell}\Phi(u_\ell)]
&=\sum_\ell\inf_{s\in\mathbb R}
[\bar p_{0\ell}\Phi(-s)+\bar p_{1\ell}\Phi(s)]\\
&=\sum_\ell\Psi(\bar p_{0\ell},\bar p_{1\ell})=H(\bar\Pi).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

This equality concerns infima and does not require a finite minimizing score. For any $\epsilon>0$, choose each $u_\ell$ within $\epsilon/N$ of its scalar infimum:

\[
\Psi(\bar p_{0\ell},\bar p_{1\ell})
\le\bar p_{0\ell}\Phi(-u_\ell)+\bar p_{1\ell}\Phi(u_\ell)
\le\Psi(\bar p_{0\ell},\bar p_{1\ell})+\epsilon/N.
\]

Summing and letting $\epsilon\downarrow0$ proves \eqref{app:score-infimum}, including zero class masses. The infima are finite because score zero gives

\[
0\le\Psi(p_0,p_1)\le\Phi(0)(p_0+p_1),\qquad
0\le H(\bar\Pi)\le\Phi(0)\sum_{i,\ell}\bar\Pi_{i\ell}=\tau B.
\]

The final equality uses the row masses and $\sum_iq_i=2$. Applying the nonnegative-variable rule to the class and coordinate prices gives

\begin{equation}\label{app:price-elimination}
\begin{aligned}
\inf_{\lambda\ge0}\sum_k\lambda_k(\tau\theta_k-\mathbf1^{\top}\bar\beta_k)>-\infty
&\iff\mathbf1^{\top}\bar\beta_k\le\tau\theta_k,\\
\inf_{w_0,w_1\ge0}\sum_k w_k^{\top}[\bar\beta_k+\theta_k\bar\mu-A_k(\bar\Pi)]>-\infty
&\iff A_k(\bar\Pi)\le\bar\beta_k+\theta_k\bar\mu,
\quad k=0,1.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

All linear terms then have infimum zero, leaving exactly $H(\bar\Pi)$ as the dual objective. We can also eliminate $\bar\beta_k$: it must cover the positive movement left after subtracting $\theta_k\bar\mu$, while its total is at most $\tau\theta_k$. Coordinatewise,

\begin{equation}\label{app:beta-minimum}
\begin{aligned}
\bar\beta_{kj}\ge0,\quad
A_{kj}(\bar\Pi)\le\bar\beta_{kj}+\theta_k\bar\mu_j
&\iff\bar\beta_{kj}\ge(A_{kj}(\bar\Pi)-\theta_k\bar\mu_j)_+,\\
\mathbf1^{\top}\bar\beta_k\le\tau\theta_k
&\implies\sum_j(A_{kj}(\bar\Pi)-\theta_k\bar\mu_j)_+\le\tau\theta_k.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Conversely, when the last inequality holds, choosing $\bar\beta_k=(A_k(\bar\Pi)-\theta_k\bar\mu)_+$ satisfies both price conditions. Therefore its elimination is exact, and the dual becomes

\begin{equation}\label{app:normalized-dual}
\begin{aligned}
\max_{\tau,\bar\Pi,\bar\mu\ge0}\quad&H(\bar\Pi)\\
\text{subject to}\quad&\bar\Pi\mathbf1=\tau q,\qquad\tau+\bar\mu^{\top}z=1,\\
&\sum_j(A_{kj}(\bar\Pi)-\theta_k\bar\mu_j)_+\le\tau\theta_k,
\quad k=0,1.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The remaining variables describe transported masses and their correction. Test scores and prices have all disappeared.

\subsection{Show that the dual reaches the primal value}
The derivation above gives a lower bound. To prove equality and dual attainment, we construct a strictly feasible point after removing coordinate prices that are necessarily zero. The primal minimum is attained by Appendix~\ref{app:attainment}; its existence argument used the bound $B$, but no constraint $\eta\le B$ is added to \eqref{eq:risk}.

A zero coordinate forces both its prices to vanish:

\[
z_j=0\implies0\le\theta_0w_{0j}+\theta_1w_{1j}\le\eta z_j=0
\implies w_{0j}=w_{1j}=0.
\]

Delete these fixed prices and the redundant inequalities they create. Choose $0<\alpha<1$ and use, on the remaining coordinates,

\begin{equation}\label{app:strict}
\begin{aligned}
u&=0,\qquad a_i=\Phi(0)+1,\qquad\lambda_k=1,\qquad w_k=\alpha z,\\
J&=2[\Phi(0)+1]+\theta_0+\theta_1,\qquad
\eta=J+1+\alpha(\theta_0+\theta_1).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

These choices leave positive slack in every remaining constraint:

\begin{equation}\label{app:strict-residuals}
\begin{aligned}
\Phi(y_i u_\ell)-a_i-\mathbf d_{i\ell}^{\top}w_{c_i}
&=-1-\alpha\mathbf d_{i\ell}^{\top}z<0,\\
w_{kj}-\lambda_k&=\alpha z_j-1<0,\\
J-\eta&=-1-\alpha(\theta_0+\theta_1)<0,\\
\theta_0w_{0j}+\theta_1w_{1j}-\eta z_j
&=-(J+1)z_j<0\quad(z_j>0).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Moreover $w_{kj}>0$ on retained coordinates and $\lambda_k>0$. Thus finite-dimensional convex strong duality gives an attained dual maximum equal to $v(z)$ for the reduced problem.

Restore each deleted coordinate by setting

\[
z_j=0:\quad\bar\beta_{kj}=0,\qquad
\bar\mu_j=\max_k A_{kj}(\bar\Pi)/\theta_k.
\]

Then $\bar\beta_{kj}+\theta_k\bar\mu_j\ge A_{kj}(\bar\Pi)$ for both classes, the $\bar\beta_k$ budgets do not increase, and $\bar\mu_jz_j=0$ leaves normalization unchanged. Hence the full dual \eqref{app:normalized-dual} also attains the value $v(z)$.

\subsection{Normalize the masses to obtain the fraction}
The rows of $\bar\Pi$ sum to $\tau q$. Dividing both $\bar\Pi$ and $\bar\mu$ by $\tau$ will restore the original source weights and remove $z$ from their feasible set. The objective scales by the same factor: for $\tau>0$,

\begin{equation}\label{app:homogeneity}
\begin{aligned}
\Psi(\tau p_0,\tau p_1)
&=\inf_s\tau[p_0\Phi(-s)+p_1\Phi(s)]
=\tau\Psi(p_0,p_1),\\
H(\tau\Pi)&=\sum_\ell\Psi(\tau p_{0\ell},\tau p_{1\ell})=\tau H(\Pi).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

We must first ensure that an optimum has $\tau>0$. If $\tau=0$, its objective is zero:

\[
\bar\Pi\mathbf1=0\implies\bar\Pi=0\implies H(\bar\Pi)=\sum_\ell\inf_s0=0.
\]

A positive feasible objective is obtained by sending every source to one target $\ell_*$. With $e_{\ell_*}$ the vector having a single one at that target, let $\Pi=q e_{\ell_*}^{\top}$. The target then receives unit mass from each class. Convexity and score zero give

\[
\Phi(-s)+\Phi(s)\ge2\Phi(0),\qquad
H(\Pi)=\Psi(1,1)=2\Phi(0)=B>0.
\]

Correct all coordinate movements and scale back to the normalized dual by taking

\begin{equation}\label{app:positive}
\mu_j=\max_k\frac{A_{kj}(\Pi)}{\theta_k},\qquad
\tau=\frac1{1+\mu^{\top}z},\qquad
\bar\Pi=\tau\Pi,\qquad\bar\mu=\tau\mu.
\end{equation}
\[
\begin{aligned}
\bar\Pi\mathbf1&=\tau q,\qquad
\tau+\bar\mu^{\top}z=\tau(1+\mu^{\top}z)=1,\\
A_k(\Pi)-\theta_k\mu&\le0
\implies(A_k(\bar\Pi)-\theta_k\bar\mu)_+=0,\\
H(\bar\Pi)&=\tau H(\Pi)=\tau B>0.
\end{aligned}
\]

This excludes $\tau=0$ at an attained optimum. For any normalized feasible point with $\tau>0$, define $\Pi=\bar\Pi/\tau$ and $\mu=\bar\mu/\tau$. Its constraints become

\begin{equation}\label{app:constraint-scaling}
\begin{aligned}
\bar\Pi\mathbf1=\tau q&\iff\Pi\mathbf1=q,\\
A_k(\bar\Pi)&=\tau A_k(\Pi),\\
(A_k(\bar\Pi)-\theta_k\bar\mu)_+
&=\tau(A_k(\Pi)-\theta_k\mu)_+,\\
\sum_j(A_{kj}(\bar\Pi)-\theta_k\bar\mu_j)_+\le\tau\theta_k
&\iff\sum_j(A_{kj}(\Pi)-\theta_k\mu_j)_+\le\theta_k.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

For $\tau>0$, positive-part scaling uses $(\tau t)_+=\tau t_+$. These are precisely the certificate conditions \eqref{eq:certificate}, with no $z$. The one equation that still contains $z$ determines the scale and hence the objective:

\begin{equation}\label{app:scaling}
\begin{aligned}
\tau+\bar\mu^{\top}z=1
&\iff\tau(1+\mu^{\top}z)=1
\iff\tau=(1+\mu^{\top}z)^{-1},\\
H(\bar\Pi)&=\tau H(\Pi)=\frac{H(\Pi)}{1+\mu^{\top}z}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Thus the denominator comes from restoring the row masses to $q$. It is positive because $\mu,z\ge0$. Conversely, any pair satisfying \eqref{eq:certificate} returns to the normalized dual by choosing this $\tau$ and setting $(\bar\Pi,\bar\mu)=\tau(\Pi,\mu)$. Equation \eqref{app:constraint-scaling} verifies its movement and row conditions, and $\tau(1+\mu^{\top}z)=1$ verifies normalization. The objective is the same in both directions, proving \eqref{eq:ratio} and attainment of its maximum.

\subsection{Connect the representation to selection and global cuts}
For binary $z$, the risk model and the original finite problem have the same value. Starting from a feasible point of \eqref{eq:finite}, set $\eta=J$ and $w_k=\lambda_kz$. Then

\begin{equation}\label{app:binary-forward}
\begin{aligned}
0\le w_k&\le\lambda_k\mathbf1,\\
\theta_0w_{0j}+\theta_1w_{1j}
&=z_j\theta^{\top}\lambda\le z_jJ=z_j\eta,\\
a_i+\mathbf d_{i\ell}^{\top}w_{c_i}
&=a_i+\lambda_{c_i}\mathbf d_{i\ell}^{\top}z.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The risk-price inequality \eqref{eq:prices} proves the middle line. Thus all risk-model constraints hold, and $v(z)\le V(z)$.

In the reverse direction, a feasible risk-model point satisfies

\begin{equation}\label{app:binary-reverse}
\begin{aligned}
z_j=0&\implies\theta_0w_{0j}+\theta_1w_{1j}\le0
\implies w_{0j}=w_{1j}=0,\\
z_j=1&\implies w_{kj}\le\lambda_k,\\
&\hspace{1em}\Longrightarrow\quad w_k\le\lambda_kz.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
\[
\Phi(y_i u_\ell)\le a_i+\mathbf d_{i\ell}^{\top}w_{c_i}
\le a_i+\lambda_{c_i}\mathbf d_{i\ell}^{\top}z.
\]

Here $\theta_k>0$ forces zero prices on unselected coordinates, and $\mathbf d_{i\ell}\ge0$ proves the second inequality. Therefore $(a,u,\lambda)$ is feasible in \eqref{eq:finite}, with $V(z)\le J\le\eta$. Taking the minimum gives $V(z)\le v(z)$ and hence equality.

Finally, fix one feasible certificate pair $(\Pi,\mu)$. To obtain a global tangent, we need its fraction to be convex in $z$. Set $h=H(\Pi)\ge0$, $r(z)=1+\mu^{\top}z>0$, and $f(z)=h/r(z)$. Differentiating one coordinate at a time gives

\begin{equation}\label{app:derivatives}
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial r}{\partial z_j}&=\mu_j,\qquad
\frac{\partial f}{\partial z_j}
=h[-r(z)^{-2}]\mu_j=-\frac{h\mu_j}{r(z)^2},\\
\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial z_m\partial z_j}
&=-h\mu_j[-2r(z)^{-3}]\mu_m
=\frac{2h\mu_j\mu_m}{r(z)^3},\\
\nabla^2f(z)&=\frac{2h\mu\mu^{\top}}{r(z)^3}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

For any direction $b\in\mathbb R^D$,

\[
b^{\top}\nabla^2f(z)b
=\frac{2h}{r(z)^3}(\mu^{\top}b)^2\ge0.
\]

Hence $f$ is convex, and its tangent at any $z^s\in[0,1]^D$ satisfies

\[
f(z^s)+\nabla f(z^s)^{\top}(z-z^s)\le f(z)\le v(z).
\]

The certificate conditions do not depend on $z$, so \eqref{eq:ratio} takes the maximum over a fixed family of convex functions; $v$ is therefore convex as well. Together with binary equality and the attained fraction representation, this proves Theorem~\ref{thm:dual} and identifies the lower functions used by the algorithm.

\section{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:global}: valid bounds and finite termination}
\label{app:algorithm}
The proof follows the algorithm. First, each full solve supplies a cut, a safe fraction and
an actual test bound. Next, the filter forces any later evaluation to be
sufficiently different from every earlier one. Counting these different
selections proves finite termination.

\subsection{A full solve supplies reliable lower functions}
At the evaluated subset $z^s$, use the feasible test bound $R_s$ and
certificate fraction $f_s$ from Section~\ref{sec:algorithm}. Write the
allowed numerical downward shift as $e_s\ge0$:

\[
\begin{aligned}
f_s(z)&=\frac{h_s}{1+(\mu^s)^\top z},
&0\le h_s&\le H(\Pi^s),\\
\ell_s&=f_s(z^s)-e_s,
&\ell_s\le V(z^s)&\le R_s,\qquad R_s-\ell_s\le\delta.
\end{aligned}
\]

The shift is included in the inner gap $\delta$; it does not change the
fraction's gradient. Convexity and Theorem~\ref{thm:dual} give, for every
$z\in[0,1]^D$,

\[
\begin{aligned}
g_s&=\nabla f_s(z^s)
=-\frac{h_s\mu^s}{[1+(\mu^s)^\top z^s]^2},\\
L_s(z)&=\ell_s+g_s^\top(z-z^s)\\
&=f_s(z^s)+\nabla f_s(z^s)^\top(z-z^s)-e_s\\
&\le f_s(z)-e_s\le f_s(z)\le v(z).
\end{aligned}
\]

Thus the cut is valid globally, even though its value and slope come
from one fully evaluated subset. The safe fraction uses the same
certificate and the same certified anchor. To verify its numerator, set
\[
\begin{aligned}
d_s&:=1+(\mu^s)^\top z^s>0,\\
n_s&:=[\ell_s]_+d_s
=\left[\frac{h_s}{d_s}-e_s\right]_+d_s
=[h_s-e_sd_s]_+,\\
0&\le n_s\le h_s,\\
F_s(z)&=\frac{n_s}{1+(\mu^s)^\top z}
\le\frac{h_s}{1+(\mu^s)^\top z}
=f_s(z)\le v(z).
\end{aligned}
\]
The gradient remains $\nabla f_s(z^s)$; it is not recomputed from $n_s$.
For $e_s=0$, convexity gives $F_s=f_s\ge L_s$.
With a downward shift, both functions remain valid but neither need
dominate the other everywhere. Retaining their maximum covers both cases.
On binary subsets $v=V$, so retaining these lower functions and actual
feasible tests gives
\[
a_t(z)=\max\{0,L_s(z),F_s(z):s=1,\ldots,t\}\le V(z),\qquad
\mathrm{OPT}\le U_t=\min\{B,R_1,\ldots,R_t\}.
\]

\subsection{The master certifies stopping or supplies a candidate}
First verify that the extra master bound $\eta\le U_t$ preserves its
minimum. Let $\widehat z_t$ be the subset of the retained feasible test
with risk bound $U_t$; at $t=0$ use the constant test on any full-budget subset.
Then
\[
\min_{z\in\mathcal Z_d}a_t(z)
\le a_t(\widehat z_t)\le V(\widehat z_t)\le U_t.
\]
The selection set is finite, so $a_t$ attains its minimum. At a minimizer,
choosing $\eta=a_t(z)$ satisfies both sets of lower constraints and
$0\le\eta\le U_t$. Conversely, those constraints force
$\eta\ge a_t(z)$. Hence \eqref{eq:master} still has objective
$\min_{z\in\mathcal Z_d}a_t(z)$. The retained test also supplies a feasible
master point $z=\widehat z_t$, $\eta=U_t$.

The full-budget equality does not change this minimum either.
For any $z\in\mathcal Z_d$ with fewer than $d$ selected coordinates,
add coordinates to obtain $z'\ge z$ with $\mathbf1^\top z'=d$.
Since $g_s\le0$, $n_s\ge0$ and $\mu^s\ge0$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
L_s(z')-L_s(z)&=g_s^\top(z'-z)\le0,\\
F_s(z')&=\frac{n_s}{1+(\mu^s)^\top z'}
\le\frac{n_s}{1+(\mu^s)^\top z}=F_s(z),\\
a_t(z')&\le a_t(z).
\end{aligned}
\]
Every selection can therefore be completed without increasing its lower
value, so
\[
\min_{z\in\mathcal Z_d}a_t(z)
=\min_{\substack{z\in\mathcal Z_d\\\mathbf1^\top z=d}}a_t(z).
\]
The original risk is likewise nonincreasing, since a test on the smaller
subset is still a test on the larger one. Thus imposing full budget is
also consistent with the original optimization problem.

To obtain the linear model, use $n_s$ defined above. Positivity of the
denominator gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
\eta\ge F_s(z)
&\iff \eta\bigl[1+(\mu^s)^\top z\bigr]\ge n_s\\
&\iff \eta+\sum_j\mu_j^s(\eta z_j)\ge n_s\\
&\iff \eta+(\mu^s)^\top b\ge n_s.
\end{aligned}
\]
For $0\le\eta\le U_t$, the product bounds in Section~\ref{sec:algorithm}
force $b_j=\eta z_j$ at both binary values:
\[
\begin{aligned}
z_j=0:&\quad 0\le b_j\le0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad b_j=0=\eta z_j,\\
z_j=1:&\quad b_j\le\eta,\quad b_j\ge\eta\quad
\Longrightarrow\quad b_j=\eta=\eta z_j.
\end{aligned}
\]
Conversely, $b_j=\eta z_j$ satisfies all three bounds: for $z_j=0$ they
reduce to $b_j=0$ and $0\ge\eta-U_t$; for $z_j=1$ they reduce to
$b_j=\eta$ and $\eta\le U_t$. This proves exact equivalence of the
linear and fractional masters at every binary selection.
The implementation may also include the following implied rows:
\[
\sum_jb_j=\eta\sum_jz_j=d\eta,\qquad
(\mu^s)^\top z\ge n_s/U_t-1\quad(U_t>0).
\]
The first follows by summing the products; the second follows from
$n_s\le\eta[1+(\mu^s)^\top z]\le U_t[1+(\mu^s)^\top z]$.
They do not change the binary feasible set.

The master therefore minimizes $a_t$ over all feasible selections. Since
$a_t\le V$, its certified lower bound is also a lower bound on the true
optimum:

\[
\mathrm{LB}_t\le\min_{z\in\mathcal Z_d}a_t(z)
\le\min_{z\in\mathcal Z_d}V(z)=\mathrm{OPT}\le U_t.
\]

Earlier master bounds remain valid when new cuts and fractions are added, because
$a_{t+1}\ge a_t$. Taking the largest certified bound therefore preserves
this interval. If $U_t-\mathrm{LB}_t\le\varepsilon$, the retained test has
risk at most $U_t\le\mathrm{OPT}+\varepsilon$.

Otherwise, every accepted candidate must satisfy the algorithm's filter

\begin{equation}\label{app:eligible}
a_t(z)\le U_t-\varepsilon/2.
\end{equation}

When the small pool has no such candidate, the master returns $z_{\mathrm M}$
with $a_t(z_{\mathrm M})-\mathrm{LB}_t\le\xi$. Using the updated lower
bound and $\xi\le\varepsilon/4$ gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
a_t(z_{\mathrm M})
&\le\mathrm{LB}_t+\xi
<U_t-\varepsilon+\xi\\
&\le U_t-3\varepsilon/4
<U_t-\varepsilon/2.
\end{aligned}
\]

Thus the master either certifies stopping or provides an eligible
candidate. Prediction only orders eligible candidates and requires no
accuracy assumption in this proof.

\subsection{The filter forces evaluated subsets apart}
The idea is that a cut is accurate at its own evaluated subset. A later
candidate can pass the filter only if this cut drops by a definite amount.
First, a previously evaluated $z^s$ cannot be selected again:

\[
a_t(z^s)\ge L_s(z^s)=\ell_s
\ge R_s-\delta\ge U_t-\delta
>U_t-\varepsilon/2.
\]

To quantify how far the next subset must move, use the data weights $M_j$
and distance $\rho$ defined in Section~\ref{sec:algorithm}. The row masses
of a feasible certificate bound each movement coordinate:

\[
\begin{aligned}
0\le A_{kj}(\Pi)
&=\sum_{i\in I_k,\ell}\Pi_{i\ell}d_{i\ell,j}\\
&\le\sum_{i\in I_k}\Bigl(\sum_\ell\Pi_{i\ell}\Bigr)
                       \max_\ell d_{i\ell,j}\\
&=\sum_{i\in I_k}q_i\max_\ell d_{i\ell,j}\le\theta_kM_j.
\end{aligned}
\]

If $\mu_j>M_j$, replacing it by $M_j$ keeps
$(A_{kj}(\Pi)-\theta_k\mu_j)_+=0$ for both classes. Clipping therefore
preserves \eqref{eq:certificate} and decreases the denominator. Hence we
may use $0\le\mu^s\le M$ before forming $f_s$ and its cut. Choosing score
zero in \eqref{eq:psi} also gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
0\le h_s\le H(\Pi^s)
&\le\Phi(0)\sum_\ell(p_{0\ell}+p_{1\ell})=2\Phi(0)=B,\\
0\le -g_{s,j}
&=\frac{h_s\mu_j^s}{[1+(\mu^s)^\top z^s]^2}\le BM_j.
\end{aligned}
\]

Now consider an earlier evaluation $s<t$ and the subset $z^t$ accepted
after $t-1$ completed evaluations. The retained test gives
$U_{t-1}\le R_s$, and the retained cut gives $L_s\le a_{t-1}$.
The inner accuracy and filter thus imply

\[
\ell_s\ge R_s-\delta\ge U_{t-1}-\delta,\qquad
L_s(z^t)\le a_{t-1}(z^t)\le U_{t-1}-\varepsilon/2.
\]

Subtract and use the bounded nonpositive gradient:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\varepsilon/2-\delta
&\le\ell_s-L_s(z^t)
=-g_s^\top(z^t-z^s)\\
&\le\sum_j(-g_{s,j})(z_j^t-z_j^s)_+\\
&\le B\sum_jM_j(z_j^t-z_j^s)_+
\le B\rho(z^t,z^s).
\end{aligned}
\]

In the second line, removing negative contributions can only increase
the sum. Consequently every pair of completed evaluations satisfies

\[
\rho(z^t,z^s)\ge r_\varepsilon
=\frac{\varepsilon/2-\delta}{B}>0.
\]

\subsection{Count the distinct patterns and conclude}
Only the largest coordinate weights are needed to distinguish such
separated subsets. Sort $M_{(1)}\ge\cdots\ge M_{(D)}$ and retain the first
$s_\varepsilon$ coordinates, where

\[
s_\varepsilon=\min\left\{s\in\{0,\ldots,D\}:
\sum_{j>s}M_{(j)}<r_\varepsilon\right\}.
\]

If two evaluated subsets had the same binary pattern on these coordinates,
they could differ only on the omitted coordinates. Write $z_{(j)}$ for the
entry at the coordinate with weight $M_{(j)}$. Since binary entries differ
by at most one,

\[
\rho(z,z')=\sum_{j>s_\varepsilon}M_{(j)}|z_{(j)}-z'_{(j)}|
\le\sum_{j>s_\varepsilon}M_{(j)}<r_\varepsilon,
\]

contradicting separation. The tail inequality is strict because the
separation bound allows equality.

Each completed evaluation therefore has a different retained pattern.
Selecting exactly $i$ retained coordinates gives
$\binom{s_\varepsilon}{i}$ patterns, and the budget allows at most $d$. Hence

\[
T_\varepsilon\le
\min\left\{|\mathcal Z_d|,
\sum_{i=0}^{\min(d,s_\varepsilon)}\binom{s_\varepsilon}{i}\right\}
\le2^{s_\varepsilon}.
\]

If the algorithm did not stop, the pool or master would supply another
eligible candidate. Completing its solve would require a further distinct
pattern, so continuation cannot exceed this finite count. It follows that
$U_t-\mathrm{LB}_t\le\varepsilon$ is reached and the returned test has risk
at most $\mathrm{OPT}+\varepsilon$.

$T_\varepsilon$ includes the first full evaluation; numerical refinements
at one selected subset count as part of that evaluation. The conclusion
assumes the required inner and master accuracies are attained. A premature
budget exit instead returns the available feasible test and valid bounds.
All numerical certificate and comparison errors are included in the stated
accuracy budgets.

\section{Loss formulas for Section~\ref{sec:examples}}\label{app:losses}
Every row in Section~\ref{sec:examples} is obtained by minimizing the same scalar objective. Write $F(s)=p_0\Phi(-s)+p_1\Phi(s)$, so
$\Psi(p_0,p_1)=\inf_s F(s)$. First take $p_0,p_1>0$; the axes are treated below.
When the minimum is attained, $s^*$ denotes a minimizing scalar score.

For half hinge, $\Phi(s)=(1+s)_+/2$. If $s>1$, replacing it by $1$ keeps $\Phi(-s)=0$ and reduces
$\Phi(s)$. If $s<-1$, replacing it by $-1$ keeps $\Phi(s)=0$ and reduces
$\Phi(-s)$. It is therefore sufficient to minimize on $[-1,1]$:
\begin{equation}\label{app:hinge}
\begin{aligned}
F(s)&=\frac{p_0(1-s)+p_1(1+s)}2
=\frac{p_0+p_1}2+\frac{p_1-p_0}2s,\\
\Psi(p_0,p_1)&=
\begin{cases}F(-1)=p_0,&p_1\ge p_0,\\F(1)=p_1,&p_1<p_0,\end{cases}
=\min(p_0,p_1).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The choice of endpoint follows from the sign of the linear coefficient;
when $p_0=p_1$, every score in the interval is optimal.

For logistic, $\Phi(s)=\log(1+e^s)$. Differentiate the two terms separately:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac d{ds}\bigl[p_0\log(1+e^{-s})\bigr]
&=-\frac{p_0e^{-s}}{1+e^{-s}}=-\frac{p_0}{1+e^s},\\
\frac d{ds}\bigl[p_1\log(1+e^s)\bigr]
&=\frac{p_1e^s}{1+e^s},\\
F'(s)=\frac{-p_0+p_1e^s}{1+e^s}=0
&\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad -p_0+p_1e^{s^*}=0\\
&\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad e^{s^*}=p_0/p_1,\quad s^*=\log(p_0/p_1).
\end{aligned}
\]
The derivative changes from negative to positive at $s^*$, so this is the
minimum. Substituting both logarithms gives
\begin{equation}\label{app:logistic}
\begin{aligned}
1+e^{-s^*}&=(p_0+p_1)/p_0,
&1+e^{s^*}&=(p_0+p_1)/p_1,\\
\Psi(p_0,p_1)&=p_0\log\frac{p_0+p_1}{p_0}
+p_1\log\frac{p_0+p_1}{p_1}\\
&=p_0[\log(p_0+p_1)-\log p_0]
+p_1[\log(p_0+p_1)-\log p_1]\\
&=(p_0+p_1)\log(p_0+p_1)-p_0\log p_0-p_1\log p_1.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

For exponential, $\Phi(s)=e^s$. A square proves the lower bound and identifies equality:
\begin{equation}\label{app:exponential}
\begin{aligned}
F(s)-2\sqrt{p_0p_1}
&=\left(\sqrt{p_0}e^{-s/2}-\sqrt{p_1}e^{s/2}\right)^2\ge0,\\
\sqrt{p_0}e^{-s^*/2}=\sqrt{p_1}e^{s^*/2}
&\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
e^{s^*}=\sqrt{p_0/p_1},\quad s^*=\tfrac12\log(p_0/p_1),\\
\Psi(p_0,p_1)&=F(s^*)=2\sqrt{p_0p_1}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

For squared hinge, $\Phi(s)=(1+s)_+^2$. The same clipping argument restricts the score to
$[-1,1]$. Expanding and completing the square gives
\begin{equation}\label{app:squared-hinge}
\begin{aligned}
F(s)&=p_0(1-s)^2+p_1(1+s)^2\\
&=(p_0+p_1)s^2+2(p_1-p_0)s+(p_0+p_1)\\
&=(p_0+p_1)\left(s-\frac{p_0-p_1}{p_0+p_1}\right)^2
+(p_0+p_1)-\frac{(p_0-p_1)^2}{p_0+p_1},\\
(p_0+p_1)-\frac{(p_0-p_1)^2}{p_0+p_1}
&=\frac{(p_0+p_1)^2-(p_0-p_1)^2}{p_0+p_1}
=\frac{4p_0p_1}{p_0+p_1},\\
s^*&=\frac{p_0-p_1}{p_0+p_1}\in[-1,1],
\qquad\Psi(p_0,p_1)=F(s^*)=\frac{4p_0p_1}{p_0+p_1}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The nonnegative square vanishes at this feasible score, which proves the minimum.

All four losses in this appendix satisfy $\Phi(s)\to0$ as $s\to-\infty$. Thus zero mass from one class lets its opposite score direction drive the remaining loss to zero. Nonnegativity supplies the matching lower bound:
\[
\begin{aligned}
0\le\Psi(p_0,0)&\le p_0\Phi(-s)\longrightarrow0\quad(s\to+\infty),\\
0\le\Psi(0,p_1)&\le p_1\Phi(s)\longrightarrow0\quad(s\to-\infty),\\
\Psi(p_0,0)&=\Psi(0,p_1)=\Psi(0,0)=0.
\end{aligned}
\]
For half hinge and squared hinge, the first zero is already attained at
$s\ge1$, and the second at $s\le-1$. For logistic and exponential with a
positive remaining mass, it is only an infimum as the score tends in the
stated direction. The closed formulas use $0\log0=0$ and assign the
squared-hinge value zero when both masses are zero.

\section{Risk predictions for the selection rule}\label{app:prediction}
A completed inner evaluation has the certified gap $R_s-\ell_s\le\delta$, so

\[
0\le R_s-V(z^s)\le R_s-\ell_s\le\delta.
\]

Thus each completed solve provides an approximate observation of the best
risk for its binary subset. We use these observations to rank the subsets
passing the global-cut filter.

\subsection{Predicting risk and uncertainty}
Normalize risks by $B$ and define the similarity kernel

\[
\begin{aligned}
r_t&=(R_1/B,\ldots,R_t/B)^\top,\\
k(z,z')&=\tfrac14\exp\!\left(-\frac{\|z-z'\|_1}{\max(1,d)}\right),\qquad
\nu=\max\{10^{-8},(\delta/B)^2\}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Here $r_t$ collects the normalized observed risks. The kernel $k$ decreases
with the number of coordinates on which two subsets differ. The fixed
parameter $\nu$ stabilizes the computation; it does not assume a probability
law for the inner-solve errors. Let $I_t$ be the identity matrix and
$\mathbf1_t$ the vector of ones. Define

\[
K_t=(k(z^s,z^r))_{s,r=1}^t,\qquad
k_t(z)=(k(z^1,z),\ldots,k(z^t,z))^\top,\qquad
A_t=K_t+\nu I_t.
\]

The matrix $K_t$ stores similarities among evaluated subsets, and the
vector $k_t(z)$ compares a proposal with those subsets. To obtain a prediction,
let $Y$ model the normalized observations and $F$ the proposal's normalized
risk. Our calculation seeks

\[
m_t(z)=B\,\mathbb E[F\mid Y=r_t],\qquad
\sigma_t(z)^2=B^2\operatorname{Var}(F\mid Y=r_t).
\]

Use prior mean $1/2$ and the following joint Gaussian model. Positivity of
its covariance matrix is verified below.

\[
\begin{pmatrix}Y\\F\end{pmatrix}
\sim\mathcal N\!\left(
\begin{pmatrix}\tfrac12\mathbf1_t\\\tfrac12\end{pmatrix},
\begin{pmatrix}A_t&k_t(z)\\k_t(z)^\top&\tfrac14\end{pmatrix}
\right).
\]

Subtract a linear prediction based on $Y$ from $F$. Choosing its coefficient
as $k_t(z)^\top A_t^{-1}$ cancels the covariance with $Y$, leaving the residual

\[
E=F-\tfrac12-k_t(z)^\top A_t^{-1}(Y-\tfrac12\mathbf1_t).
\]

The residual has mean zero. Its covariance with $Y$ and its own variance are

\[
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Cov}(E,Y)
&=k_t(z)^\top-k_t(z)^\top A_t^{-1}A_t=0,\\
\operatorname{Var}(E)
&=\tfrac14-2k_t(z)^\top A_t^{-1}k_t(z)
 +k_t(z)^\top A_t^{-1}A_tA_t^{-1}k_t(z)\\
&=\tfrac14-k_t(z)^\top A_t^{-1}k_t(z).
\end{aligned}
\]

Because $(E,Y)$ is jointly Gaussian, zero covariance gives independence.
Conditioning on the completed observations therefore leaves the mean and
variance of $E$ unchanged:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E[F\mid Y=r_t]
&=\tfrac12+k_t(z)^\top A_t^{-1}(r_t-\tfrac12\mathbf1_t),\\
\operatorname{Var}(F\mid Y=r_t)
&=\operatorname{Var}(E)=\tfrac14-k_t(z)^\top A_t^{-1}k_t(z).
\end{aligned}
\]

Returning to the original risk scale gives

\begin{equation}\label{app:gp-prediction}
\begin{aligned}
m_t(z)&=B\left[\tfrac12+k_t(z)^\top A_t^{-1}
                         (r_t-\tfrac12\mathbf1_t)\right],\\
\sigma_t(z)&=B\sqrt{\tfrac14-k_t(z)^\top A_t^{-1}k_t(z)}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

For $t=0$, use $m_0=B/2$ and $\sigma_0=B/2$. These are model predictions
for the selection rule, not certified confidence bounds. The global guarantee
in Theorem~\ref{thm:global} does not assume that the model describes the true
risk correctly.

To verify the covariance used above, each binary coordinate contributes the
matrix with positive eigenvalues:

\[
\begin{pmatrix}
1&e^{-1/\max(1,d)}\\e^{-1/\max(1,d)}&1
\end{pmatrix},\qquad
1\pm e^{-1/\max(1,d)}>0.
\]

The kernel matrix on all binary subsets is the tensor product of these
matrices, scaled by $1/4$. Hence the kernel matrix for any finite list of subsets is positive
semidefinite. In the joint covariance above, $\nu I_t$ adds a positive
quadratic-form contribution whenever the observation component is nonzero;
a vector with only its last component nonzero has positive variance $1/4$.
The joint matrix is therefore positive definite. This makes $A_t$ invertible
and its conditional variance strictly positive.

\subsection{Updating the predictor with little overhead}
Maintain the lower-triangular Cholesky factor $C_t$ of $A_t$. One new
completed observation adds one row and column:

\[
A_t=C_tC_t^\top,\qquad
A_{t+1}=\begin{pmatrix}
A_t&k_t(z^{t+1})\\k_t(z^{t+1})^\top&\tfrac14+\nu
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Retain $C_t$ and seek a new lower-triangular factor with row $v_*^\top$ and
positive diagonal $c$. Block multiplication gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
C_{t+1}C_{t+1}^\top
&=\begin{pmatrix}C_t&0\\v_*^\top&c\end{pmatrix}
  \begin{pmatrix}C_t^\top&v_*\\0&c\end{pmatrix}\\
&=\begin{pmatrix}
A_t&C_tv_*\\v_*^\top C_t^\top&v_*^\top v_*+c^2
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Matching this matrix to $A_{t+1}$ determines the new entries:

\[
\begin{aligned}
C_tv_*&=k_t(z^{t+1}),\\
v_*^\top v_*+c^2&=\tfrac14+\nu
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
c=\sqrt{\tfrac14+\nu-v_*^\top v_*}.
\end{aligned}
\]

The square-root argument is positive because $A_{t+1}$ is positive definite.
Thus the update needs one triangular solve and one appended row.
For predictions, compute the observation weights $\alpha_t$ using two
triangular solves, and compute $v$ for each proposal:

\[
C_tp=r_t-\tfrac12\mathbf1_t,\qquad
C_t^\top\alpha_t=p,\qquad C_tv=k_t(z).
\]

Here $p$ is a forward-solve intermediate. The first two equations give $A_t\alpha_t=r_t-\mathbf1_t/2$; the last gives
$k_t(z)^\top A_t^{-1}k_t(z)=v^\top v$. Substitution into
\eqref{app:gp-prediction} yields

\[
m_t(z)=B[\tfrac12+k_t(z)^\top\alpha_t],\qquad
\sigma_t(z)=B\sqrt{\max\{0,\tfrac14-v^\top v\}}.
\]

Updating the factor and predicting a bounded batch cost $O(t^2)$ arithmetic
operations, with $O(Dt)$ additional work for each similarity vector. The
kernel parameters remain fixed, so no hyperparameter optimization is needed
between inner solves. The maximum with zero only removes numerical roundoff.
